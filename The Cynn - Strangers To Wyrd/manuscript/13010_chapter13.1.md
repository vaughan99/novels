# Chapter Thirteen: Stories for the Road

## Part One: The Stories We Tell Each Other

When darkness finally settled over the road, One-Eye drew the first cask of ale from the wagon.

“We have traveled far enough,” he declared. “I shall name you Lovely. Now come sit beside me, my lovely.”

Tor found wooden cups among the provisions. Ketill opened the cask, and the company gathered around the fire with cups of ale.

Ketill quietly sat and brought forth a shoulder haversack. He removed a few horns and a small bit of toothed steel. He slowly started drawing the steel across the horn lengthwise.

_Whrrrta Whrrrta_...

A section would come off. One would have a deep curve, another would have less. Each one in turn, he took his knife and trimmed the edge flat, and placed it on a stack of other horn sections.

Then he would sip his ale.

Tor watched him with amusement. "What are you doing?"

Ketill stopped his sawing and looked up. "My trade."

"Why not take a hammer to the horn? Would be faster."

"Want pieces. Different pieces."

"For what?

"Skin."

"Skin?"

"For battle."

"Armor?"

"Learn this from horse masters of steppe. Learn many ways of battle-skin. This no take forge. Strong like iron... if iron had horns."

"And you learned this from who?"

"Horse men from east. Big land. Much travel. Much time by fire. Not much to do... except make battle-skin."

_Whrrrta Whrrrta_...

For a while, they spoke of horses, roads, and how much distance they had gained on the fyrd. The ale passed from hand to hand.

Then Murdock looked across the flames at Cynewulf. Murdock crumpled onto the ground, landing on his rump with a big smile and a bigger cup of ale next to Cynewulf.

“I suppose this is the proper time to tell them about the naked giant!”

Cynewulf lowered his cup.

“No.”

Elswyth sat forward. “There was a giant of a wolf-man in the South Pastures yesterday. He was there last night when the wolves came. Was that the one?”

“Yes,” Cynewulf said.

Murdock turned toward the others. “The giant followed Cynewulf to the church last night.”

Cynewulf protested. “He was not a giant.”

Murdock continued, “He stood three heads taller than Cynewulf."

Ketill corrected without looking up. "No. Two. Wolf-head not count."

Murdock shook his head.

"There they were in the street between the Forum and the church, weapons drawn.”

One-Eye took a gulp of ale. “Was he naked yet?”

Murdock laughed. “Of course he wasn’t naked yet. Let me finish.”

He took another drink.

“Cynewulf cursed the giant so thoroughly, so viciously that he stripped off his armor, dropped his weapons, and fled. Then the church bells began to ring.”

Elswyth drank. “Where did he go?”

Everyone looked at Cynewulf.

“He just... ran.”

Cynewulf felt the weight of Wodbora at his belt, the hilt pressing into his hip.

Murdock continued. “Aye, he ran. Then everyone heard the bells and came running. The priest looked at the armor, the shield, and the sword. He was the first to ask where the naked Viking had gone. Then the villagers kept him there for hours, demanding the proper prayer against naked evil!”

The company laughed.

Cynewulf drank.

“That is one account.”

“It is the accurate account,” Murdock said.

Tor was incredulous. "So who rang the bells if everyone was asleep?"

Mischief widened Murdock’s eyes. “Everyone had a different answer. Some said valkyries rang them because they expected to take a soul. Others blamed a local ghost... a thing called the _Coaler-Wight_.”

He took another drink.

“Me, I think the bells grew a sense of humor.”

Murdock laughed so hard he almost spat out his ale.

The laughter trailed and the conversation lowered, allowing the crackle of the fire to have its say.

Tor smiled at Cynewulf and took a sip. "So, Saxon, do you always let Scots tell your stories for you? Or do you have another?"

Cynewulf stared into the fire.

"I'm not one for stories. Reality is far too harrowing."

Cynewulf raised his head. “Osric, how did your own fyrd make you a thrall?”

Osric gave Murdock a weary look.

“You have been telling stories.”

“Aye lad.”

Elswyth looked between them. “His own men captured him?”

“They had the wrong idea,” Osric said.

“That hardly improves it,” Cynewulf replied.

Osric settled against his saddle on the ground, and his smile disappeared.

“The first night of winter was the worst of many bad nights. We had a visitor who took grain from the _eorth-hord_. My wife and I disagreed over what... _it_ was. That distracted us from the syrup.”

One-Eye stopped in mid-drink. “Syrup?”

“Elderberries,” Osric said. “They went undercooked. My family and I ate it that first night.”

Tears welled in his eyes.

Murdock laid a hand on his shoulder. “Saints’ blood save ye, lad. I had no idea.”

Osric took a moment to compose himself.

"The... _thing_... that stole our grain, was no ordinary thing." He studied the eyes of the listeners. "Ealhild knew what it was. I should have trusted her. It got... offended."

Tor's eyes narrowed. "_It_ got angry?"

Osric looked into the fire. "It tore the roof off the house."

The company sat stunned.

"By the time the fyrd came to forage for supplies, the... _thing_... had saved my life, rebuilt the byre, sorted the animals, and heated it for God's sake."

A scowl crept across Osric's face. He stared deeper into the fire, as though his stare were the only thing keeping it hot.

"The men of the fyrd saw the wreck of my house. They accused me of going mad. Then one of them noticed the snow barrow I had created for my family. He pulled away the door. He said that I failed them. That I let them die. I punched him for it. But that wasn't the worst of it."

Osric took a long draw of ale.

“Galindo sent men into the byre to collect chickens and whatever livestock they could take for the campaign. They saw what the _thing_ had done. Every animal was fat. The animals in the other fyrdmen's byres had been reduced to skin and bone by the failure of the fall harvest and the blasted winter. But not mine.

“They became convinced that, in my madness, I had cared for the animals while killing my own wife and children. They accused me of stealing grain from the surrounding farms to keep the beasts fed while I let my own family starve. They accused me of things so vile that only witchcraft or divine punishment could explain the destruction of the roof.

“To them, it was a winter curse. An ice-borne hate-fever. Unnatural. Evil.

“Galindo quieted the men, but his questions became harder to answer, and I could not tell him about the _thing_. He had no choice but to call for a witan, only there was no time.

“So I was given into Murdock’s keeping. As was my entire byre. They spared nothing. It’s all... gone.”

Murdock narrowed his eyes. “For that first week, your _thing_ provided our every meal, lad. Except the four milkers... Galindo had them counted with the rest, but I told him they’d slow the train and bruise their udders before we reached Leicester. Your cheese-making neighbor took them into his herd. The one they call Leofstan. He’s keeping them for... the outcome of the witan I imagine.”

Osric stared into the fire, his face like stone.

Tor shook his head. "Well I don't believe in witches and fairy magic. I can't explain what happened to you, but you are _not_ insane to me. I see a _man_ down on himself, a _man_ who fought a wolfpack and won, and a _man_ who is willing to risk everything to return to a nightmare. If I can't drink to that, I shouldn't drink at all."

The others agreed.

If doubt remained among them, no one gave it voice.

The cask grew lighter.

Tor spoke of selling herring pies on the docks of York. He spoke of watching two sailors fighting over how to mend the nets. They then each took a handpie, and they tried to fight each other with them. One smashed into the first sailor's nose. The other took his hand-pie and slapped the second sailor's cheek with it, breaking two teeth.

"In the end, they made up. And the handpies still needed a knife to open, and still tasted wonderful."

One-Eye pretended to be slapped upside the cheek, passing out on the ground, landing inches away from Elswyth's knee. He looked up at her and smiled, and sat back up instantly.

Elswyth looked at him with a wry smile. "Well, One-Eye, let's learn your real name. What story have you to tell?"

One-Eye's hand drew to his cross, as he felt the texture of the iron between his fingers. He unknowingly flicked a fingernail across a bit of rust as he began.

---

Huginn: “There once was a man from Nantucket...”

Muninn: “Absolutely not.”

---

"There once was a man from Cornwall.

"His name was Talan. His father kept one of the king's woods. Not every deer belonged to the king, but the great stags did. To take one without leave was to steal from the king's own table.

"So Talan learned deer before he learned women, which was perhaps where his troubles began."

Elswyth's smile widened.

"He knew where they drank. Where they bedded..."

Elswyth laughed. "Are you talking about the deer or the women?"

One-Eye ignored her.

"He could tell a hind from a stag by its tracks and how long ago something passed by the bend of a fern.

"His father taught him never to chase a stag through the wood. Watch. Wait. Learn where it wants to go, then arrive there first.

"His father was quiet in the forest.

"Talan learned to be quiet there too.

"On the Lord's day, when the hunt did not keep them away, they went to Mass.

"Talan learned to be quiet there as well.

"But the king's hall was neither forest nor church."

One-Eye grinned.

"There, he learned everything else.

"When they brought home one of the king's stags, there was ale, meat, songs sung badly by men who thought they sang well, and women who discovered a young man silent beneath an oak could make up for it after sunset.

"Talan liked the hall.

"The hall liked Talan.

"Years passed. His father died, and Talan inherited the wood.

"He had his father's cottage, his bow, his church, and the same paths under the same trees. The priest still complained when he came to Mass with mud on his boots.

"He had everything a man needs.

"Which is usually when God notices you've grown comfortable."

One-Eye glanced down at his cross.

"One autumn the king came hunting.

"He brought half his hall, most of whom were useless beneath trees. Men who shouted when they should whisper. Men who stepped on every dry branch God had scattered before them.

"He also brought his niece.

"She was..."

"Pretty?" Tor offered.

"No."

One-Eye looked offended.

"Fascinating. In the way the rainbow is... fascinating."

Elswyth rolled her eyes.

"She rode well."

Elswyth interrupted. "Rode well? Now I know this is _NOT_ a story about me..."

"She knew enough about hunting to know when to be..." One-Eye cast his eye sideways toward Elswyth, "..._silent_, and little enough to ask questions worth answering.

"Talan answered them.

"Then she began asking questions that had very little to do with deer.

"Talan answered those too."

"Poor man," Elswyth said.

"Helpless."

One-Eye sighed.

"Talan put the king onto a great stag. The king struck it well enough that everyone afterward agreed it had been an excellent shot.

"Talan agreed most loudly.

"They returned to the hall.

"There was ale. There was singing. There was dancing.

"And the king's niece discovered the keeper of the wood was considerably less quiet beside a fire than beneath an oak."

Elswyth looked directly at him.

One-Eye ignored her.

"Perhaps she touched his hand.

"Perhaps he touched hers.

"Perhaps she kissed him."

One-Eye considered.

"Perhaps he was an innocent man assaulted without warning."

"I'm sure he remembered it that way," Elswyth said.

"Almost certainly."

The men laughed.

"He also remembered kissing her back.

"The king remembered that part too.

"The next morning, Talan was dragged from his bed.

"The king asked whether he had put his hands upon the king's own.

"Talan said the king's own had put her hands upon him first."

Cynewulf closed his eyes.

"Ah," Murdock said.

"Yes," One-Eye said. "Not his wisest answer."

The laughter faded.

"The king ordered Talan's right hand taken.

"The hand had touched what was above its station.

"So the hand would go."

One-Eye's fingers stopped against the cross.

"His father had taught him the bow with that hand. He had dressed his first deer with it. Crossed himself with it. Lowered his father into the ground with it.

"The priest heard.

"He came before the king and did something brave.

"He contradicted an angry man with a crown.

"He said Talan was no corrupter. He had known him since childhood. Talan attended Mass whenever the forest allowed it. He confessed. He brought meat when the church needed it. He carried wood for widows in winter.

"'Punish foolishness if you must,' the priest said. 'But do not make a useless man from a useful one.'

"The king listened.

"He knew what the bow meant to Talan.

"So the king showed mercy."

One-Eye smiled without humor.

"He ordered them to take an eye instead.

"'Leave him his hands. Let him hunt for his bread. But he will never hunt my woods again. And let him see with one eye that he could _not_ see with two.'

"So they held Talan down.

"He prayed.

"Not very well.

"There are prayers priests teach you, and there are prayers men discover when someone approaches their face with a knife.

Cynewulf winced at the truth of those words.

"Talan discovered several."

The fire cracked.

"When it was done, the priest washed his face.

"Talan asked why God had allowed it.

"The priest said he did not know.

"Talan asked why he should speak to a God who would not answer.

"The priest said, 'Silence is not absence.'

"Talan disliked that answer."

Cynewulf gave the faintest smile.

"Before Talan left, the priest came to him again.

"He brought an iron cross.

"It wasn't pretty. A smith had made it. Hammer marks still showed upon it, and one arm was shorter than the other.

"The priest placed it in Talan's hand.

"'I cannot give you back your wood. I cannot give you back your eye. I cannot tell you why God did not stop them.

"'But they can drive you from my church. They cannot drive Christ from you.'"

One-Eye's fingers closed around his own cross.

"Talan left Cornwall.

"And he discovered the world was full of people willing to carry a man a little way.

"A fisherman carried him across water in exchange for pulling nets.

"A widow fed him after he killed the fox taking her hens.

"A merchant gave him a place on his cart because Talan knew which tracks beside the road belonged to deer and which belonged to men hiding in bushes.

"A priest gave him a floor beside a chapel.

"A woman once gave him considerably more."

Elswyth snorted.

"Talan learned their names. Their stories. Who hated whom. Who loved whom. Who claimed to hate someone because admitting the other thing frightened them.

"He hunted when he needed meat.

"He worked when he needed silver.

"He stole when circumstances became... challenging."

Murdock laughed.

"And that was when Talan understood people."

One-Eye looked around the fire.

"They are boats.

"You meet one at a shore. Perhaps you pay. Perhaps you work. Perhaps you drink together until neither remembers whose boots are whose.

"But for a while, you travel together.

"Then another shore comes.

"You get out.

"The boat goes somewhere else.

"And so do you."

Elswyth's expression had changed.

"A boat can save your life.

"You can love a boat.

"You can remember every board in it years after you've stepped ashore."

One-Eye shrugged.

"But only a fool tries to live in one."

For several moments there was only the fire.

Elswyth looked at him.

"And what happened to Talan?"

One-Eye raised his cup.

"He became much more careful around the nieces of kings."

"No."

Elswyth's eyes narrowed.

"What happened to him?"

One-Eye smiled.

"He kept traveling."

Elswyth looked at the cross in his hand, then at his missing eye.

"Was Talan you?"

One-Eye kissed the iron cross.

"That wasn't your question. Besides I have three other versions. In one, Talan is driven from Exeter by Æthelstan. Boring one, that."

Cynewulf's ears perked. "Did you say Æthelstan?"

One-Eye poked the fire with a stick. "Yes. I did. But like I said, it's a boring tale. The kid was forced along with the other Cornish to leave Exeter by the King of the Saxons himself. There was fighting when they were driven out, and somewhere in the mess he lost his eye. It was then he decided it was better to pretend to be Saxon than to be known as Cornish. His English was good enough for the ruse, and before long, no one much cared where he had come from... so long as he brought meat to the tables of the thegns."

Cynewulf shook his head. "Gods and kings lift their fingers and mountains rise or fall. And it is we that must pick up that which they knock down."

One-Eye nodded, then smiled. "I prefer the version where he fought a boar naked with a stick after a bath in a moonlit pond. But I didn't choose that one, for we've already had a story with _naked evil_." He giggled.

One-Eye picked up a pebble and tossed it near Ketill. It landed on his neat stack of horn plates.

Ketill looked up from his wares, and realized it was time he took a turn.

Ketill smiled at One-Eye for a long moment, then spat into the fire. He jabbed with two fingers to the northeast.

“Finn-men tell this. Every water have spirit. Own name. Own keeper. Not same, never same. One want bread. One want bit silver. One want no sound. One want true name.

“I go north once from Novgorod, with Finn guide, with traders. Black lake there. No bird. No wind. Water flat like iron.

“Old Finn say, ‘When water ask, give true name. Then water let pass.’

“We laugh little. But we do it.

“One by one, we stand by shore. We speak names. Water quiet. Good. We cross.

“But Sven he fool. He grin. Water ask name.

“Sven say, ‘I am Hiisi.’

“Finn guide go white. Tell him, ‘No. Do not give that name. That evil name.’

“Sven laugh. He say, ‘Water have spirit. I have spirit too. Let us see which one stronger.’

“Lake say nothing then.”

“Night come. We sleep by shore.

“In dark, from reeds, voice come.

“Soft voice. Wet voice.

“It call name Sven gave.

"Hiisi.

"Once.

“Hiisi!

“Twice,

"Hiisi!

“Third time. Sven wake half-sleep and say, ‘Aye?’

“That all.”

Ketill looked around the fire and he smiled.

“Morning, blanket empty. Wet tracks go down to lake. No tracks come back.

“So. When water ask name, give true name. Sven gave evil name. Water believe him.”

One-Eye laughed and lifted his cross toward the fire. “Thank You, Lord. I have been drunk enough to argue with trees, carts, and one very stubborn goat. But I've never disagreed with a lake. Please see that I never have to!”

Ketill smiled at One-Eye. "You invent name for self? One day... have to."

At last, Murdock looked toward Elswyth.

“And what stories do coalers tell?”

Elswyth sat against one of the wagon wheels, turning the smooth piece of coal between her fingers.

“Not stories meant for drinking.”

“One hopes those are the best kind,” One-Eye said.

Elswyth looked beyond the firelight.

“There was once a charcoal burner who heard knocking beneath one of his clamps.”

The company quieted.

“He thought a root had split in the heat. Then the knocking came again.”

She struck the coal softly against the wheel.

Once. _Tink._

Twice. _Tink._

A third time. _Tink_.

Cynewulf stared at the Alexander stone in her hand, but said nothing.

“The burner opened the clamp. The coals were cold, but smoke poured from the earth. Inside stood a little man wearing a red cap.”

One-Eye lowered his cup.

“The little man asked to warm himself beside the coals. He promised good stories and fine meals in return.”

Tor smiled faintly. “A fair bargain.”

“So the burner thought.”

Elswyth watched the fire.

“The redcap took a hare from the burner’s snare and opened its throat, but did not cook it. He washed a cap from his hand in the blood until the cloth turned dark. He promised that once his family's caps were soaked, they would arrive. And then he left.”

Murdock stopped smiling.

“The next night, he returned. Then the next. Each time he brought another cap. Each time he promised that soon his family would come.”

Cynewulf held his cup without drinking.

“The burner barred his hut. He set traps around the pit and waited with his axe.”

One-Eye leaned nearer the fire. “Did the redcaps come?”

“Yes. It turns out they all came to watch every time, hiding up high in the trees. But only one was sent to the hut each night, with a borrowed cap to wet. But this time, the gnome needed to wet his _own_ cap.”

Elswyth closed her fingers around the coal.

“The redcap reached for yet another rabbit. And then the earth began to smoke. The coaler clutched his axe and waited in the corner of his home. The redcap looked at its feet.”

The fire snapped.

“It rose through the ground. It was the Coaler-Wight.”

Ketill watched her without blinking.

“It was a smoking apparition with orange eyes and sooted hands. It seized the murder-gnome by the ankles and dragged him beneath the earth. He kicked. He screamed. Foul poetry spat from his lips as the fiery coals filled his mouth.”

Elswyth lowered her voice.

“The other redcaps watched from the trees. They didn't mourn the loss of their own... they laughed until there was nothing left above the earth but his cap, now scorched.”

Murdock raised his eyebrows. "I thought redcaps favored castles..."

Elswyth replied, "That's where they keep their court. But every now and then, they slink off to the local woods to find someone to wet their caps."

Elswyth stared into the flames.

"Unless the Coaler-Wight stops them."

Silence settled over the hollow.

One of the horses stamped beyond the firelight.

One-Eye looked down between his boots.

“Why did the Coaler-Wight take him?”

“Perhaps the spirit recognized an even more foul spirit than itself,” Elswyth said. "But coalers know... the Coaler-Wight protects its own."

Tor took a long drink. Murdock rubbed his beard. “Coalers tell that tale to children?”

“No,” Elswyth said with a smile. “They tell it to one another... and to themselves.”

One-Eye shifted closer to the fire.

Cynewulf studied Elswyth, but she kept her eyes on the coal in her hand.

Tor looked at Ketill as he reached for the cask. He was fascinated by the depth of Ketill's story knowledge.

"So Ketill, I'm no judge of ghost stories. Which story was scarier? Yours or Elswyth's?"

"Elswyth. Water come alive, you run to land. Ground come alive, you run to water? You die anyway."

Tor lifted the cask.

“Well. Something has been drinking this while we listened.”

“The Coaler-Wight,” One-Eye said with a glum face.

The laughter returned, though not as loudly as before.

"What did I say?" One-Eye protested. As they rose, they clapped One-Eye on the shoulder and he smiled faintly.

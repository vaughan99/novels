# Chapter Fourteen: The Return

At dawn, Cynewulf spread a cloak across the ground and weighed its corners with stones, laying the Cynn's map on it to use as a reference. Osric crouched beside him and drew the north road with a charred stick in the mud to discuss their plan.

_Thwack._

Nearby, Elswyth loosed another bolt from the crossbow into the grain sacks while Murdock watched with the anxious attention of a man counting every shot in silver.

Ketill pointed north of Cynewulf’s marks. “Fyrd must cross here?”

Osric nodded. “The Wear. Dere Street crosses near Binchester. After a week of rain, it may be the only crossing fit for carts.”

“Other ford?” Ketill asked.

“Perhaps in summer,” Murdock said. “Not now. The lesser fords will be running deep enough to carry away a horse.”

Cynewulf drew another line, bending away from the road and returning below the river.

“Then we do not cross elsewhere. We leave Dere Street here, cut across the high ground, and regain it south of Binchester.”

Murdock studied the line. “No lad. One week of rain, Dere Street is the only road these wheels will want to see.”

Osric looked up at Murdock. "Yes. No more mud. Please."

Cynewulf frowned. "Well, then we pass the fyrd on Dere Street. Hopefully by night."

Osric shook his head. "If Galindo hears a wagon pass in the night, he'll ride up to make sure it wasn't the baggage train he's expecting. He'll know us."

One-Eye smiled. "A ruse then?"

Tor shook his head. "He's knows all of our faces."

_Thwack._

Elswyth walked up, crossbow in hand.

"Not mine," she whispered.

One-Eye's smile grew.

“We don’t need to fool Galindo for miles. We only need to fool him for a few moments.”

Cynewulf looked around the group, mischief in his eye.

“Have I ever told you the story of Saint Odile-of-the-Silt?”

The group collectively shook their heads.

“Good. Because what I’m going to ask you next, you won’t have the good sense to refuse.”

Cynewulf searched for spare linen. Finding none, he cut a strip from the canvas and tied it around his eyes.

“I’m asking you all to go blind.”

He turned his covered face toward Elswyth.

“Except Elswyth.”

Her gaze moved from the blindfold to the horses.

“No, no. I hate horses. I know what's coming next.”

Cynewulf smiled beneath the linen, raising his nose so that his eye could spy Elswyth.

“You have worked out the plan remarkably quickly.”

"But why can't a blind man ask for alms. I could be blind too? In the wagon."

One-Eye started cutting more canvas before the plan was decided. "We need you to occupy his attention. Smile at him. Show your golden hair."

She had spent half of her life hiding her hair and her face, and the one-eyed man just asked her to invert her life for a few moments. She swallowed hard.

“And if he asks me to marry him, what then?”

One-Eye kept cutting canvas. “Tell him you’ve taken vows.”

Cynewulf lifted the edge of his blindfold. “You have. As Sister Edith. Every church named 'Saint Odile-of-the-Silt' has a Sister Edith.”

Elswyth stared at him. "Do they now?"

Cynewulf smiled sheepishly. "Fine. I've run this ruse once. The name's worked before."

“I hate Sister Edith. Name the horse Sister Edith for all I care. I'm not doing it.”

One-Eye looked toward the mare.

“You heard her, Sister Edith.”

The horse shook its mane.

“She hates it too,” Elswyth said.

“Then you already have something in common,” said Cynewulf.

Cynewulf took the blindfold completely off. "Do you have a better plan?"

Elswyth put the crossbow in Murdock's hands.

"I suppose _Sister Edith_ has no need for steel bows. But I'm not flirting with the man."

“Begging..." One-Eye completed his blindfold. He removed his eye-patch, and put on the full stretch of cloth on his face. "... is not flirting.”

“You told me to smile and show my hair.”

One-Eye smiled and turned to Elswyth. “Persuasive begging?”

“Flirting.”

One-Eye tossed the canvas in the direction of Osric.

“Try not to think about it.”

Elswyth stared at him.

“I hope you walk into a tree.”

One-Eye laughed. “Truly, is that how a sister of Saint Odile-of-the-Silt treats one of her afflicted?”

Murdock raised his hand. "And which of us blind will be driving the wagon?"

Everyone stared. Nobody had an answer.

"Aye. I guess the wagon be in God's hands. Just checking."

For two days, they followed Dere Street north.

On the third morning, the tail of the fyrd appeared ahead.

It was time for the ruse.

Murdock, whose red hair made him immediately recognizable, lay in the bed of the wagon. He clutched his side and faced away from the fyrd.

The others donned their blindfolds.

Elswyth did not.

_I’m the only sighted person in a band of idiots._

Elswyth held the leader line connected to the next horse’s reins, guiding the pathetic company of blind horsemen and their single cart. Cynewulf rode next behind her, peering at the road through a narrow slit beneath his blindfold.

“This…” Elswyth whispered. “…is **not** going to work.”

“Shh. It will if you let it.”

Elswyth's blind company moved faster than the fyrd column. The farmers carried lighter sacks and led fewer goats than when they had left Leicester, but their feet were wearier.

As the procession of the blind drew alongside the rear of the column, some fyrdmen removed their caps. Others stared resolutely ahead.

Elswyth’s heart pounded as the rhythmic roll of the beast beneath her refused to yield to her demands. She felt like a fly clinging to its back.

_This is insufferable._

“Make way for…” she called. “…the blind. Make way for the poor.”

Murdock released a false groan.

“Oiii!”

One-Eye coughed from his horse... a sharp, punctuated cough intended to catch Murdock’s attention.

Murdock revised his illness.

“Uhhhh…”

Elswyth handed the leader line to Cynewulf. Ahead, she spotted Galindo upon his horse.

She braced herself.

She urged her horse forward. Her knuckles whitened around the reins as she steered it to the left and pulled alongside him.

Then she drew back her cowl.

“Alms for the blind, lord?”

_There. I said it._

Galindo regarded her with a discerning eye.

“Where is your company going in such a hurry, and without a proper watch?”

Elswyth had prepared this answer. The words still felt wrong as they passed her lips.

Cynewulf and the others began passing Galindo.

“We are eager to get home, lord. Our pilgrimage… a long way from Frankia.”

Galindo’s eyes widened.

“Frankia? You have travelled far.”

Elswyth bit down, trying to steady herself upon the shifting horse.

“We are from… Saint Odile-of-the-Silt. I tend to the blind.”

Galindo’s brow tightened.

“All this way and no proper guard? Please... it would be my pleasure to have you follow us as far as Hexham.”

“No!” Elswyth blurted.

Galindo looked at her.

“I mean... no, lord. We have a leper among us.”

“A leper?”

“Who is blind.”

“Which one?”

“In the wagon, lord. Brother Osten…”

Her mind struggled to complete the false name.

“…tatious.”

“Brother Ostentatious.” Galindo considered it. “Ost-en-tay-shus? Ost-and-taze-ee-us? How is that pronounced?”

“Yes, lord. That one.”

“Which one?”

“…the last one.”

“A peculiar name.”

“He is of… Roman descent, lord.”

“That name... Ost. Is he from the east?”

“I think so, lord. He said he was from Cornwall.”

“Cornwall is west, child.”

“It is where he pointed on the map, lord.”

“I see.”

“I thought it best not to press him, lord.”

“I see.”

Galindo glanced toward the wagon.

“Well, the offer stands. Let the leper travel last in the train.”

“I couldn’t, my lord. Saint Odile demands that the blind cannot see the leper, and so… we cannot see the sickness. We are immune to it.”

“Immune?”

“That is what the abbot said, lord.”

“Abbot?”

“In Frankia.”

“I see.”

“It is a test of faith, lord.”

“Very well. And the bandits?”

“I suppose it is a test of faith for them as well, lord.”

“Not to rob you?”

“I think robbers do not prey upon lepers, lord.”

“I see.”

Galindo reached into his purse and produced three silver pennies. He reached across and bade Elswyth open her hand.

He pressed the coins firmly into her palm.

“Tell Brother Ostentatious that our prayers are with him.”

“Yes, lord.”

Galindo gathered his reins.

“I must say, for someone who has ridden to and from Frankia, it is horrible that nobody taught you how to properly ride a horse. Loosen your hands, child. Hold with your thighs, not the horse’s mouth. He knows the road better than you do.”

“Lord?”

“Loosen your hands and close your thighs against his sides. You cannot balance because your hips are not anchored. And you are confusing the horse by holding the reins so tightly.”

Elswyth looked down at the reins and her thighs. She realized what he was saying was true.

“Thank you… lord?”

"Christ see you home. I must tend my fyrd."

Then he turned back toward the tail of his column, checking on the stragglers.

Impossibly, the ruse had worked.

And that was how Elswyth learned to ride a horse.

Impossibly.

She loosened her hands and urged the horse forward, drawing alongside Cynewulf. Then she took back the leader line.

“I think it worked.”

Cynewulf shook his head. “Brother Ostentatious… really?”

“You named me. You named the horse. But you neglected to name anyone else!”

“You know what One-Eye is going to say.”

“Uhh… don’t tell me.”

“He will want an identity at Saint Odile’s. Perhaps Father Half-Sight?”

“Half-wit, more like!”

One-Eye called from behind them, “I heard that!”

Once over the next hill, they abandoned their ruse. They all had a good laugh.

Elswyth realized she did not trust horses any more than before.

But she trusted herself on one now... a little more than before.

And that was why she finished the day on the back of a horse.

The day came that the river Wear lay before them. They could see the water, but they could not see the ford. They still had a bit of travel before the crossing.

The first arrow struck the road before them.

It stood quivering in the mud.

Men rose from the brush on either side of Dere Street. Some held hunting bows. Others carried spears, axes, and old steel. Their clothing was a mixture of wool, leather, and whatever armor they had taken from men less fortunate than themselves.

Several fistfuls of rocks were thrown at the wheels of the wagon. The wheel lurched, then rolled backward.

The horses startled. Murdock pulled hard against the reins while Tor and Ketill drew their weapons.

One-Eye quickly slipped the bow from his shoulder and nocked an arrow.

“Do not loose,” Cynewulf said.

One-Eye froze with the bow drawn.

A man stepped into the road ahead of them. He wore a dented helmet without a nose-guard and a mail shirt missing most of one sleeve. A fox pelt hung across his shoulders, though the animal’s head had been cut away.

He looked over the horses, the wagon, and the armed company.

“Well,” he said. “I am a fisherman of men. And look at what my net has caught! Generous.”

Osric’s hand rested on Barrow’s Edge.

“We have no quarrel with you.”

“That is often what rich travelers say before discovering one.”

The bandit leader pointed his sword toward the wagon.

“Leave the horses, the silver, and half the provisions. You may keep your weapons if you promise not to use them.”

One-Eye leaned toward Murdock.

“That seems fair.”

Murdock gave him a hard look.

The bandit leader took another step forward.

Cynewulf dismounted.

The man stopped.

Cynewulf pushed back his hood.

For a moment, no one moved.

The bandit’s sword lowered slightly.

“Cynewulf?”

Several men on the ridge shifted. One whispered something to another.

Cynewulf studied the leader.

“Hacca.”

Hacca smiled, but there was no warmth in it.

“I heard you were dead.”

“So did I.”

The bandits began lowering their bows.

Hacca looked past Cynewulf toward the company.

“These yours?”

“They travel with me.”

“That was _not_ my question.”

Cynewulf’s expression did not change.

Hacca glanced toward the men on the ridges and lowered one hand.

The remaining bows came down.

Murdock loosened his grip on the reins but did not release it. Tor kept his axe in hand.

Hacca approached carefully.

“You look worse than when I last saw you.”

“And you look alive.”

“Because I learned from you.”

Cynewulf’s eyes narrowed.

Hacca’s smile faltered.

"Last we saw of you was the mill road.”

Cynewulf’s jaw tightened.

One of the younger bandits called from the ridge.

“Was that where he nailed the reeve’s men beneath their own wagon?”

Cynewulf turned his head toward the voice.

The young man disappeared behind the brush.

Hacca swallowed.

“They lived,” Cynewulf said.

“For a while.”

Cynewulf took one step closer.

Hacca retreated before he could stop himself.

The movement was small, but everyone saw it.

Cynewulf spoke quietly, pointing to the stones in the road.

“Move the stones.”

Hacca looked toward the wagon again. His eyes rested on the spare horses, the grain sacks, and the casks.

Then he looked back at Cynewulf.

“We could ride with you.”

“No.”

“You do not even know what I am offering.”

“I know.”

“We have twelve men here. More nearby. Archers who can hunt, men who can separate silver from other men.”

“No.”

Hacca’s face hardened.

“You once knew the value of men like us.”

“I knew the price.”

“And now?”

“Now I know who pays it.”

The bandit leader studied him for a long moment.

Behind Cynewulf, One-Eye slowly lowered his bow.

Hacca spat into the leaves.

“You always did enjoy pretending to become a better man, while showing us the worse of it.”

Cynewulf did not answer.

Hacca looked toward his men.

“Move the rocks.”

Several bandits descended from the ridge. They kicked the rocks from the wagon's path.

As Cynewulf returned to his horse, Hacca called after him.

“The road north remembers the name _Blackshield_. It remembers _you_.”

Cynewulf placed one foot in the stirrup.

“And so do the graves, Hacca. Do not cross our road again.”

Hacca said nothing more.

"Oh, and Hacca... mind the fyrd coming up behind us. You might want to be... absent... when they come this way."

Hacca took off his cap and wiped the sweat off his brow. He looked at his men, and smiled.

They rode in silence until Hacca and his men disappeared behind them.

One-Eye was the first to speak.

“What happened on the mill road?”

Cynewulf kept his eyes forward.

“Nothing worth retelling.”

“Hacca seemed to remember it.”

“Hacca remembers whatever keeps him alive.”

Tor rode closer.

“He was afraid of you.”

“He was afraid of what he thought _Blackshield_ might do.”

Ketill looked back toward the vanished bandits.

“Was he wrong?”

Cynewulf did not answer.

The water at the Wear was high, but the bridge was higher.

The company traveled until dusk before making camp. No one suggested opening another cask.

By the seventh day, the land had begun to look familiar to Osric.

He recognized the long rise beyond the stream. He recognized the line of ash trees following the old boundary. He recognized a standing stone half sunk into the earth, though someone had split its top since he had last passed it.

He rode more slowly.

Elswyth watched him from the wagon.

“Are we close?”

Osric nodded.

No one spoke after that.

The track climbed between fields left pale and flattened by winter. The first shoots of spring pushed through the dead grass. Water ran in narrow threads along the furrows, carrying away the last crusts of snow.

At the top of the rise, Osric stopped.

His cottage stood below.

Or what remained of it.

Half the roof was gone. One wall leaned outward, held upright by little more than stubbornness and dried mud. The door hung from a single hinge. The byre beside it stood straighter than the house, its roof patched with turf and woven branches.

No animals moved within.

The fields were empty.

Osric dismounted.

He did not tether the horse.

He walked down the hill alone.

The others followed at a distance, leading the wagon and horses quietly behind them.

Osric passed the broken threshold. He did not enter the house.

Instead, he continued toward the low rise beyond it.

The snow had nearly melted.

The nisse had covered the barrow with brush, straw, and dead grass, hiding its white shape from anyone passing along the road. But spring had found it. Water trickled from beneath the covering. The sides had begun to sink.

A pale fold of cloth showed through the thaw.

Osric stopped.

For a long while, he did nothing.

Then he knelt beside the barrow and placed both hands against it.

“I came back,” he whispered.

The others remained near the cottage.

Elswyth lowered her cowl. Cynewulf bowed his head. Murdock removed his cap and held it against his chest.

Osric began pulling away the branches.

No one asked whether he wanted help.

They simply joined him.

The barrow came apart slowly. The outer snow had softened, but the deeper layers remained hard with old cold. They cleared the brush and straw first. Then they removed the ice in pieces, setting each one away from the bodies beneath.

Osric worked without speaking.

When the last covering had been lifted away, Ealhild and the children lay together beneath their shroud.

The cold had preserved them beyond kindness.

Elswyth turned her face away.

Osric touched Ealhild’s cheek through the cloth.

Then he rose.

“Not here,” he said.

He looked toward the eastern side of the house, where the ground sloped gently above the stream.

“There.”

He crossed the field and tested the earth with his heel. The surface yielded beneath him.

Tor brought one of the shovels from the wagon.

Osric took it and drove the blade into the ground.

The soil beneath the thawed surface was dark and soft.

“It will take them,” he said.

The company retrieved the remaining shovels.

They dug until afternoon.

Osric chose a single grave wide enough for all five. Cynewulf worked beside him. Murdock and Tor traded places when their backs tired. Ketill cut through roots with an axe. One-Eye carried away soil in a broken shield. Elswyth lined the bottom with dry grass and lengths of clean cloth taken from the wagon.

No one told stories.

When the grave was deep enough, they carried the family from the failing snow barrow.

Osric lifted Ealhild.

Cynewulf carried Ingvarr, the eldest.

Tor carried Mathild. Her hair was exactly as Ealhild's.

Murdock carried Beorn. The buttons he had carved were still sewn to his tunic.

Ketill carried little Eaxl. Her wooden boar was still clutched in her hand.

They placed them together beneath the earth.

Osric knelt beside the grave.

He had prepared words during every mile north. Prayers from childhood. Fragments of Psalms. Promises made while staring into campfires.

None came.

He folded his hands.

The company did the same.

Their prayer had no spoken beginning.

It lasted through the wind moving across the field. It lasted through the creak of the wagon and the nervous shifting of the horses. It lasted until the sun lowered behind the broken cottage and cast its shadow across the open grave.

At last, Osric made the sign of the cross.

“Amen.”

The others answered softly.

They filled the grave.

When the last soil had been pressed into place, Osric set five stones along its length. He placed the largest at Ealhild’s head.

He remained there after the others returned to the wagon.

Cynewulf waited nearby.

Osric looked at him.

“I need to see the house.”

Cynewulf nodded.

“I’ll be here.”

Osric crossed the field alone. The sorted remains of his roof lay in the yard, still waiting to find their usefulness once more.

Inside, the cottage smelled of damp earth, old smoke, and rotting timber. Rain had washed soot down the walls in black streaks.

The hearth remained.

Osric knelt beside the stones.

Something dark lay between the hearth and the wall.

He reached into the debris and pulled it free.

A narrow seax rested in its sheath.

Osric stared at it.

The nisse had gathered tools, repaired the byre, sorted the animals, and hidden the snow barrow.

It had overlooked the knife. Or perhaps... it had left it for him.

The sheath was cracked but whole. Along its leather face, Beorn’s pattern remained visible beneath the dirt: two snakes twisting around a bramble.

Osric brushed it with his thumb.

Beorn had drawn the pattern first in charcoal. The snakes had looked more like worms, and the bramble had been little more than a crooked line covered in thorns. Ealhild had laughed until Beorn became angry and insisted that Osric place it on the sheath exactly as drawn.

He had done his best.

Osric drew the blade.

The narrow edge was spotted with rust, but the point remained fine. Near the base of the blade was the shallow scratch.

His thumb found it immediately.

The scratch was real.

The cottage was real.

The grave beyond the wall was real.

He closed his eyes.

“_Brēr-bita_,” he whispered.

Briar-Bite.

Beorn had named it after watching him trim pegs and carve little animals beside the hearth. To Beorn, the small blade had been more important than any sword because it made whistles, toys, buttons, wedges, spoons, and once an ugly wooden horse that the boy had treasured.

Osric returned the blade to its sheath.

Outside, the company had begun preparing the cottage yard for camp. Tor unpacked provisions. Murdock inspected the byre. Elswyth stood near the new grave, staring at the stones.

Osric approached Tor.

“May I borrow your knife?”

Tor looked at the seax in his hand.

“You appear to have found one.”

“I need a finer point.”

Tor drew a small knife from his belt and offered it hilt-first.

Osric sat upon the broken threshold. He noticed the stone, the one Ealhild had brought, was missing. Just empty earth.

He smiled. It was a weak, weary smile. He realized... the thing had taken the stone. Wherever it was... it had carried with it the two runes Ealhild painted.

The seax’s antler handle had once possessed several prongs. Most had been removed and polished flat when the grip was made. One broad flat remained beneath the place where Osric’s thumb naturally rested.

He placed Tor’s knife against it.

“What are you carving?” Elswyth asked.

Osric did not answer until the first line had been cut.

“A mark Ealhild used.”

He carved carefully.

The rune was simple, though his hand trembled. It was one of two Ealhild had painted upon the threshold stone when they first moved into the cottage. One invoked the wealth of the field, in service of Freyr. The other named the blood that belonged within it.

This was the second.

_Ancestry_.

He deepened each line until it would not disappear beneath years of handling.

When he finished, he brushed away the pale antler dust.

The rune lay beneath his thumb.

Hidden when he held the knife.

Present whenever he needed to feel it.

Osric returned Tor’s blade.

“Thank you.”

Tor examined the rune but did not ask what it meant.

Osric slid Briar-Bite into its sheath and fixed it at his belt.

Behind him, the evening light touched the five stones of the grave.

On the hill above the cottage, horses moved among the ash trees.

A dozen riders had gathered beneath the branches. Their cloaks bore the marks of the Hexham hearthguard. Spears rested across their saddles, and shields hung at their knees.

Their leader studied the wagon, the strange horses, the armed company, and the grave newly raised beside the ruined house.

He raised one hand.

The riders remained silent.

Below, Murdock called for help clearing space in the byre. One-Eye argued that Lovely should be opened in honor of the dead. Cynewulf stood at the edge of the field, looking south along the road they had escaped.

None of them looked toward the hill.

None saw the hearthguard watching.

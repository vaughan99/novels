# Chapter Three: The Past Is Risen

Elswyth was from Under-Geat, a daughter of the charcoal and the forest. She seldom mingled with the village-folk now, for she had spent half her life balanced on the sharp edge between truth and lie. Which was which depended on who you asked.

She cared little what others thought of her. The quality of her charcoal came first. Her skill at coaxing a living from the forest was not far behind. Both kept her fed, and neither cared a whit for village gossip.

She was dressed for the damp forest and the heat of the char-pits, not the thread-wise handiwork of the linen makers. Her garments were a patchwork of scavenged pieces, each stained a different shade of black. Her coaler’s apron was a stiff, heavy sheet of cowhide, blackened by greased fingers and scorched by flying sparks. A short, green wool hood and waist-cloak shielded her shoulders from the damp spring drizzle, while rough wool wraps bound her legs to protect them from the forest's grasping briars. On her feet, simple leather turn-shoes, stuffed with dry moss to ward off the lingering chill of the damp earth, kept her grounded in the Leicester mud.

In the beginning, her family did the dirty work of managing charcoal pits with wood taken from the nearby Charnwood forest. The charcoal fed the Church bell foundry and the Yardworks' lime kilns. But now she was alone. She continued that work in the still of the night, away from the eyes and ears of the villagers.

For two months that spring, the Saxon horde had made Under-Geat their home. And for two months, Elswyth watched the forest awaken, filling her snares before filling her basket. She avoided the villagers when selling to the visitors, always drawing up her cowl before offering anything for sale. Mushrooms were often traded for broth. A small rabbit could bring a loaf of bread. But a marten's pelt could buy four suppers, and its meat would be the fifth.

That morning, she took the news of the peace with dread. She had no ear for war, but she also had no love for the Danes, especially those dressed as wolves. While the Danes ruled, she preferred the call of the forest birds to the sounds of the Vikings' boasts. The Saxons had taken Leicester only to give it back. She wasn't sure that Leicester was her home anymore.

Osric's Tyne River fyrd was one of the companies encamped just outside Under-Geat. Their wagon master was a Scot named Murdock, a fire-bearded man whose first instinct was always to count: wagons, oxen, barrels, sacks, and finally people. He had seen Elswyth faithfully deliver her bounty for several weeks. And with the mud, his count of spare axles was down to a single hand. He needed a forester to prepare for the worst. Broken axles did not grow on wagons, and hungry men did not march on promises.

Murdock offered her a place with the company before the wagons departed north. She accepted before she had time to think better of it. The ruin of her coaler's hut held little worth taking: a blanket, a small mushroom knife, a cooking pot, her father's aging felling axe, her wedge-driving cudgel, an assortment of iron and wooden wedges, a coal-rake, and the twine and triggers of her traps. It was all kept in a bundle of empty charcoal sacks.

And finally, her hand found the smooth lump of coal in her pocket, polished over years of handling until it shone like black glass.

She was ready to go.

Suddenly, the bells from the bell tower began to peal.

Priests descended into the Old Frith, directing preparations for Easter Mass. They brought forth a table to serve as an altar and draped it in embroidered white linen, as though defying the ankle-deep mud beneath it.

Refugees, livestock, wagons, and even the church’s broth kitchen were moved onto the South Pastures as the priests cleared the Old Frith. Meanwhile, townsfolk streamed in from every quarter of Leicester.

The Easter procession began its journey from the Church of St. Augustine and St. Columba.

A deacon walked at the head of the procession, ringing a handbell in slow, measured strokes. The crowd quieted as the sound crossed the Old Frith.

Behind him came the processional cross, flanked by two young clerics carrying lit candles. Another bore the Gospel book high against his breast, while a thurifer walked before it, casting incense toward the altar.

Two men followed with an open, iron-bound chest faced in pieces of Roman tile. Within it lay a mound of earth beneath white linen, marked by a small wooden cross... a little tomb prepared for the risen Christ.

Last came the senior priests of Leicester’s two churches, robed for Easter Mass. Young attendants followed close behind, gathering the lower folds of their vestments above the mud.

Before the altar, woven mats of basket-willow reeds had been spread over the churned ground. The attendants guided the priests onto them, lowered the gathered cloth, and stepped aside.

A murmur passed through the gathered crowd. King Edmund, Archbishop Oda, and Archbishop Wulfstan were noticeably absent. At dawn, Edmund had ridden north with his household warriors to receive Olaf's oath at a place arranged the night before by Wulfstan. There, on the banks of the River Soar, Oda baptized Olaf while Edmund stood as his sponsor.

Elswyth made her way to the edge of the South Pastures for a better view. From the higher ground, she searched the gathering for Long-Mile and Shadow-Wife. They were the only two she cared enough about to embrace before leaving. She could find neither. With so many dead still unburied, she supposed they had more pressing work elsewhere.

Then she noticed a broad-shouldered wolf-man approaching the gathering with a larger man by his side, dressed in the same manner. Other members of their company collected along the fringes, watching the spectacle. One of the wolf-men gave a long howl. Several laughed, and others spat upon the ground. But someone had plainly ordered them not to interfere, and the peace held.

She thought she might recognize this broad-shouldered wolf. Carefully, she crept around the edge of the crowd closer.

Wulfhere and Rognvaldr watched the procession in confusion.

Rognvaldr pointed toward the chest. “What treasure lies inside their box of dirt?”

Wulfhere did not look at him. “They say their dead god rose today. The box is meant to be his tomb.”

Rognvaldr shook his head. "A god that dies and is reborn? Does this happen every year?"

Wulfhere shook his head. “They celebrate the death and return of their god. Their priests even count the years by his life.”

Rognvaldr frowned. “Since his death?”

“Since his coming, I think. Their priests argue over such things.”

“They carry a little tomb for their dead god. Imagine Thor’s reaction if we celebrated when he died.”

Wulfhere laughed. "Careful Rognvaldr. Bolts of lightning have hit men for speaking less."

Rognvaldr smiled. “Thor has better things to do..."

He blew his nose into the dirt.

"...than listen to me.”

A table nearby held wooden bowls of broth, steaming into the cold air. Rognvaldr took one without asking and drank deeply. Broth spilled from the corners of his mouth into his beard. He set down the bowl, wiped his mouth with his forearm, and belched.

Elswyth crept closer. She noticed the way the broad-shouldered wolf-man wore his beard, cut shorter on the sides and braided at the chin. It was certain. She knew this Viking. Her heart filled with hate.

Rognvaldr studied the crowd once more. He pointed toward a band approaching from the North Gate. They appeared to be late.

“Looks like we aren’t the only wild flowers in this bundle.”

The Welshmen wore green and red cloaks, their hair loose and wild. A few were bare-chested, streaks of drying water and mud still marking where they had scrubbed themselves clean. Several around them gave stray looks in their direction, but they paid no heed.

Upon entering the Old Frith, they immediately knelt and crossed themselves. Their devotion seemed more fervent than that of many already gathered.

“Look, Wulfhere. The filthy cloaks have given their hearts to the dead god.”

Rognvaldr watched as some chanted beneath their breath. Others prayed aloud. A few raised their hands toward the heavens as the priest read from the Gospel book.

Wulfhere noticed one among them who carried himself differently. He wore the same cloak, but stood with quiet reverence, his hands folded before him. He occasionally cast glances toward his cloaked companions, still kneeling in the mud. He listened intently as the Gospel was read, his lips silently forming the Latin words as they fell from the priest’s mouth. Beneath his parted cloak lay mail and a langseax.

Wulfhere pointed. “That one. He’s different. A captain, perhaps?”

Rognvaldr smiled. “Old mail. I rarely collect new mail.”

Wulfhere smiled. “Easy, friend. These barbarians are stronger than they look.”

When the communion procession began, the cloaked company rose as one.

They did not wait for the line to form. Two slipped ahead through gaps in the crowd. Another gently lifted an old man by the elbows and set him three feet to the side. One ducked beneath a woman’s outstretched arm. Another stepped onto the edge of a wagon wheel, passed behind a deacon, and dropped neatly into place before the altar.

No one was struck. No one was injured.

Several people were nevertheless left standing with their mouths agape, wondering what had just happened.

Communion was distributed. While most received it kneeling and crossing themselves in solemn reverence, the members of the cloaked company smiled broadly.

“Hungry little flowers,” Rognvaldr giggled.

Elswyth followed the direction of Wulfhere’s finger. She recognized the man beneath the green and red cloak as well. His beard had gone to salt, his hair had grown longer, and the road had carved deeper lines into his face.

_Father Cynewulf? You’re supposed to be... dead..._

More troubling was the way Wulfhere watched him.

The wolf-man’s amusement had faded. He studied Cynewulf with the same attention a hunter gave fresh tracks, noting the mail beneath his cloak, the weapon at his side, and the glances he cast toward his companions.

Elswyth drew her cowl lower.

Once the last of the cloaked men had received Communion, they rose and gathered around Cynewulf. He had remained apart, his hands folded and his head bowed.

He never approached the altar.

Together, they made their way toward the North Gate.

Wulfhere turned suddenly, his gaze sweeping toward Elswyth.

She ducked behind an old woman, nearly knocking her over, then slipped behind the line of people waiting for Communion and hurried toward the Tyne River fyrd’s wagons.

All the while, she tried to put Wulfhere’s face out of her mind.

But it would not leave her.

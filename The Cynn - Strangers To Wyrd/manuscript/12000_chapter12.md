# Chapter Twelve: Getting A Handle On Things

A meager amount of sunlight shone down through the charcoal chute to the forge-elves cavern. It was enough light for Osric to see that which had been hidden when meager forge-light was the only light he had: a metal ladder leading straight up.

At the top of the ladder, Osric pushed a number of large bricks that hid the entrance. The glare of sunlight filled his eyes. He was in the central hypocaust chamber of the bathhouse ruin, and the open archway lay before him. He retrieved the chest and pulled it up, one step at a time. It was difficult, but passed through the ladder-way with just a fingernail of space to spare. As if it were designed that way.

Elswyth had an easier time finding an exit. The _frigidarium_ had a grand entranceway which led to the Jewry Wall. She emerged into the sunlight, shading her eyes which had grown accustomed to the dark.

Osric dragged his chest along the ruined wall of the bathhouse back towards the Church, and into the same entranceway. They startled each other.

They recognized each other from the night before. Each noticed the other was dressed in new attire. Each noticed the other was dragging a chest.

Each politely pretended not to.

It was easier than explaining where any of it had come from.

The pretense became considerably more difficult when it turned out they were both dragging chests filled with the belongings of dead _Ulfhednar_.

Each chest bore the same iron emblem riveted to its lid:

_An oak sprouting from a broken anvil, with two crossed hammers beneath._

Osric stopped. "No..."

He looked at her chest.

Then at his.

She looked at his. "No..."

Their chests hit the ground with heavy thuds.

"Where did you get that?" he demanded, pointing at the insignia on her coffer.

"Why? Did something... _weird_... happen to you too?"

Osric shook his head. "Understatement."

Elswyth shook her head. "Understatement."

Osric scanned her for injuries. "Did the wolf-men find you? I see you're still alive."

Elswyth sighed. "Barely alive. And you?"

"Barely alive. Well, were your Vikings... stripped... and... sorted?"

Elswyth's jaw dropped. "Sorted? What do you mean sorted?"

Osric opened his chest. The gear lay inside exactly as he had found it, already neatly folded. He presented what he had found to Elswyth. He removed the top layer, which were a pair of Viking pants.

Elswyth frowned. "You... took the time to fold Viking pants... _neatly_?"

Osric dropped the pants back into the chest. "No!"

Elswyth's hands moved to her hips. "Well, then, who did?"

Osric slammed his chest shut. "You wouldn't believe me if I said another word."

"Well, I'm not opening mine. I didn't fold mine as neatly as you... or whoever... folded yours."

Osric closed his eyes. "I'll be fine if I never set eyes on Viking pants again."

"Complete agreement."

Osric opened his eyes and looked at their two boxes. "You know, we might move faster if I grab your handle..."

"And I grab your other handle..."

They found themselves standing one in front, one in back. Elswyth's coffer was in their right hands, and Osric's in their left.

Elswyth sighed. "Great, I'm in front. But where are we going?"

"I don't know. Anywhere but here?"

"Well, I can't go home... yet."

"Why not?"

Elswyth shook her head. "You wouldn't believe me."

"Well I can't go home, ever."

"Why not?"

Osric closed his eyes once more. "Look, can we just agree that we avoid questions for now? We just need to find a place to... think."

"Well if you need a priest, the church is just a few steps that way."

"I'm new here. Anything's fine."

"You know, the Danes own the town again."

Osric looked around for anyone watching them. No one was. "Oh. We probably shouldn't walk through town with wolf-men loot in our chests."

Elswyth nodded. "Might look... bad."

"Very bad."

Elswyth turned her head back to the frigidarium. "I spent the night in the room with the freezing pool. There's nobody there."

"Was there a place to hide two chests?"

"Yes. The place where you came from, was there a place to hide three more bodies?"

"There was a forge."

A smile crept onto Elswyth's face. "Are you thinking what I'm thinking?"

"Not sure. Can't see your face."

"If there's fuel for that forge..."

"Nooo. That doesn't feel right..."

"They're going to start to smell."

"You want to burn them?"

Elswyth's eyes narrowed. "_They tried to kill us_."

She turned, leading Osric back to the ruin of the bathhouse. "Think of it as a funeral pyre."

Osric opened his mouth to object, then found he had none.

Instead, another thought took hold.

Ealhild.

The children.

They still lay beneath the snow by the river Tyne... if they lay there at all. If the hearth in Alfheimr had not been the whole truth, the thaw would soon uncover them. If it had...

Osric tried to quicken the pace from behind Elswyth, pushing her forward. "We need to hurry."

"Hurry?"

"I need to get home."

"You said you had no home."

"My kin. They must be buried."

"Slow down!"

"And I'll need a horse."

Elswyth guided them back through the Jewry Wall entrance to the baths. They tucked the chests behind a collapsed wall so they were immediately out of view.

They dragged the dead wolf-men to the charcoal chute. Once the corpses had been lowered through the chute into the abandoned forge, they descended the ladder after them.

Elswyth marveled at the engineering of the forge. She had delivered charcoal to forges before, but most of them had been cobbled stone hearths, a ramshackle roof, and a bellows whose leather was barely holding together.

She traced her fingers on the brick lines. "This doesn't look like... what I expected."

Her eyes fell upon the dead bodies, and the pile of charcoal beneath them. "Did you see this?"

Osric had his hands on a dead Viking and started to pull it off the heap. "The charcoal?"

"This is my *singing coal*. Same color." She picked up a piece and struck it against another. "Same sound." Her eyes dropped to the older pieces buried beneath it. "And my father's..."

She pointed toward the base of the pile. The older sticks were cut to slightly different lengths, just as her father had made them.

"My father made Alexander Stones when a burn gave him something exceptional. I wanted every piece of mine to be worthy of the name."

Osric stopped pulling and looked. "The forge was lit yesterday. Suddenly... lit. Like someone else was here."

Elswyth stopped and stared at Osric. "Do _not_ blame this on the _Coaler-Wight_."

Osric blinked. "...the what?"

Elswyth thought for a long moment. She didn't want to ask this, but now she felt she had no choice.

Elswyth cleared her throat. "I'm going to ask you something, and don't laugh."

"I'm well beyond laughing. But you may ask."

"Did you find this smithy by yourself in the dark, or were you... _led_... here?"

Osric composed himself before responding. He knew this would sound ridiculous. "By a giggling child?"

Elswyth reeled backward. "NOO!"

"Does that mean something to you?"

Elswyth stared at the charcoal.

She thought of the giggling children.

The mushroom nets.

The redcap.

The forge.

The forge-elves.

Coal from her pit. Coal from her father's pit.

They hadn't followed her.

They had been here all along.

"We have to leave. Now!"

Osric looked back down at the body in front of him. "The bodies?"

"Never mind them. Those little... _giggling things_... will take care of them. We don't want to be here. Believe me."

"What? What's going on?"

"Our... _gifts_? I think I know who gave them to us."

"Who?"

Elswyth whispered, "The people who live here."

Osric whispered, "_Nobody_ lives here."

Elswyth looked around, whispering quieter still, "Yes... _nobodies_... _NO... BODIES..._ _who giggle like children._"

Osric looked at her for a long moment.

Osric dropped the body in his hands and escaped through the narrow passageways. Elswyth was right behind him.

Neither looked back as they ran to the church.

Elswyth and Osric entered the Old Frith.

The churchyard had become a refuge for every sort of soul. Farmers, merchants, craftsmen, widows, and travelers wandered among the graves and parish buildings, waiting for news, bread and broth, or simply somewhere safe to stand.

Rumors drifted through the crowd as freely as the smoke. Last night, there was a battle outside the North Gate. Some insisted the peace had been broken. Others swore it had held. The Danes now holding the town seemed far more interested in making silver from the wagons themselves than in settling the argument.

Regardless, nobody paid them any heed.

The Danes were occupied with restoring order in the streets and had little interest in the frightened crowds sheltering in the churchyard. So long as the clergy kept them peaceful, they were the Church's concern, not the soldiers'.

Osric immediately spotted Murdock by his red braids. He was gathered with what remained of the fyrd's baggage train beside one of the surviving stone piers of the Shadow Gate. Heavy oak timbers had been passed through holes bored in the old Roman masonry to brace the repaired breach. One of the great beams now served as an impromptu bench.

Murdock saw them approach and nearly dropped his bowl of broth.

"By the bells of the heavenly gates... what have we here?"

He stood and turned his head to the side.

"Osric, as I stand here... I barely recognized you. We thought you two were dead."

His attention turned to his new armor and attire, the threads still glistening in the sun. He put his hand on Osric's new wool cloak to feel the material.

"These haven't seen a single day in the mud. Good thing we don't have another axle for you to fix."

A smile crept across Murdock's face.

"Or shall I track down one fer ye so we can say we broke 'em in?"

A look of terror swept over Osric's face.

Murdock burst out laughing.

"Och, would ye look at his face!"

He clapped Osric on the shoulder.

"Easy, lad. I'm not baggage master anymore. As ye can see, there's precious little baggage left to master."

Murdock spread his hands toward the handful of sacks, pots, pans, and odds and ends that were all that remained of the train.

He shrugged.

"If you're still a thrall to the fyrd, that's between you, Galindo, and God. But..."

He gave Osric a proper head-to-toe accounting.

"I'd wager an ox to a goose egg that you're the best-dressed thrall in all of Britain."

He turned his attention to Elswyth.

"And you... what did the two of ye do, get chased by Vikings that fancied themselves tailors?"

Elswyth cleared her throat.

"Well it's strange that..."

Murdock held up his hand, shaking his head.

"Actually, save it lass. I've already heard about naked giants and angels handing out armor."

He jabbed a thumb towards Cynewulf's black-oiled mail.

"If you've got another perfectly true tale that'll sound like drunken nonsense tomorrow, I'd rather hear it after I find some ale."

Osric and Elswyth looked at each other, then at Cynewulf. His skin-cracked hand dropped onto the handle of _Wodbora_. He quickly drew his cloak over it. His face curled into a slight smirk, and his eyes veered downward, staring at the drying mud.

One-Eye leaned in. A smile crept across his face.

"So, if the angels' price for new kit is a miracle, does dropping a man at fifty paces count? I mean, they must know I have only one eye?"

The group chuckled, but One-Eye received no answer. He seemed genuinely disappointed.

They sat together a while longer, the day at last feeling like spring had made good on its promise.

Osric stood before them, his hands clasped behind his back. What came next felt like a momentous ask.

"I have urgent business in _Hagustealdsham_. I need a horse."

Murdock's mouth stood agape.

"For the blood of the saints, why boy? Ye want to get _back_ to the fyrd?"

"I can understand if none of you wish to go with me, but I have unfinished business."

Elswyth nudged Osric with her elbow.

"How far is it... to your farm?"

"Two weeks on foot, and the fyrd left yesterday. Six days comfortably, if everyone has horses."

Since arriving in Leicester with her parents as a young girl, Elswyth had been no farther than the Charnwood Forest and Leicester. As someone who had spent years pretending to be a ghost, one could almost say she had never truly belonged to either.

For the first time, she felt like a sapling in a pot... not bound to the earth.

"Well, what are we waiting for?"

Cynewulf knew this conversation well in his own mind. His own _Liturgy of the Road_ had drilled it into him from the day he left Leicester long ago.

"Provisions. Water..."

Tor interrupted.

"I think those are my concerns. I'm the quartermaster. I say two draft horses, each with..."

Murdock chimed in.

"Nobody is going to manage this company's baggage... but me! Now how many are going?"

Cynewulf stood back and smiled, as he watched Murdock and Tor argue about priorities. He watched as Osric and Elswyth talked about the wolf-men's loot, and how that could be sold quietly by Tor to the blacksmiths and the thread-makers. He watched Elswyth laugh as One-Eye teased her about missing a spot of soot in her hair.

The oddly-clothed Viking sat down next to Cynewulf.

"Group. Good."

Cynewulf smiled.

Then it struck him.

He had never asked the man's name.

Nor the name of the companion who had died beside him the night before.

"I didn't catch your name."

"Name Ketill."

"You don't look Dane. You don't sound Dane. Where are you from?"

"Aye. From where sunrise meet ice. Do you know Volga? River in east."

Cynewulf had studied a map of the known world when he was a student. He remembered it as being on the eastern edge of the world.

"Yes, I've heard of it."

"Find Volga then keep going."

Cynewulf quietly marveled at the journey this man had taken to get here.

"You're far from home. I can't even think of how long that must have taken."

"Journey not far."

Ketill looked at his boots, caked with mud, and smiled.

"Not far... if home always under feet."

"And your companion's name? Where was he from?"

"His name is _drengr_. His home is here."

He pointed to his chest.

Cynewulf was confused. He knew _drengr_ to mean a companion, an honorable man, a sworn brother.

"What did his mother call him?"

Ketill smiled.

"Name not important. Face everything."

Ketill looked to the horizon.

"He had... __Noble__... face."

Ketill looked back at Cynewulf.

"And now he where he thinks he should be."

Cynewulf thought back to the _witan-sleep_ of the ravens. His promise to honor the Viking boy whose life he took without a second thought. The boy's name was _Penance_. Ketill had done the same for his oath-sworn, and that name was _Noble_.

Cynewulf realized that people were more than souls carried by the Road, by the wind, or by the will of gods. Before all of that, they were remembered. They were loved. They were sons and daughters, brothers and sisters, parents and oath-sworn. Their histories lived on in memory. Their deeds endured in story.

Someday, others would tell his.

He wanted to be worthy of it.

The group decided to lay the remains of the two Saxon brothers and Ketill’s oath-brother in a trench dug along the western wall of the graveyard. The Saxons had little of value. Their eldest brother had shared none of his wealth with them.

Ketill honored his oath-brother according to his own burial customs. He placed beside him his drinking horn, clothing, knife, and shield, along with a small section cut from his lamellar harness. Ketill kept the remainder of the armor and took charge of his oath-brother’s coin.

Ketill did not shed tears for his companion. He stared into the open earth with hard resolve, as though the man he mourned were no longer there. He spoke a few words in a language no one present understood, then turned away.

The company left as the priests finished the burial rites for those in the trench. It was a cruelty of war that each death could not be honored with its own rite, but those present knew that war allowed no such convenience.

The company returned to the Old Frith. It was time to form a plan.

Tor and Ketill were judged the company’s best traders. Everyone helped gather and tally the goods intended for sale. 

It would take everyone to manage the transport of everything, for the haul included fragments of the gear of fourteen dead men, several of them fyrdmen, along with the remains of the baggage train.

Ketill warned them that no one would purchase the wolf-men’s headgear as it stood. The pelts, however, still had value. He cut the hides free and cast the ruined heads among the refuse piles of the Yardworks.

They carried the haul to the Maker’s Wall and began with the armorers and smiths.

There were common axes, Wulfhere’s Dane axe, and Rognvaldr’s arming sword. They had four intact byrnies and the hoard of mail taken from Rognvaldr. Leicester was crowded with others selling the spoils of war, but buyers were nearly as plentiful. War reduced good equipment to rust, splinters, and torn rings with remarkable speed.

They left the armorers carrying considerably more silver than iron.

Next came the cloth merchants, cobblers, and leatherworkers. Belts, boots, cloaks, and serviceable clothing became silver in their turn.

The remains of the baggage train contained excess rope and canvas. Those were sold with the rest, leaving the chests empty. The chests were stoutly made, iron-bound, and worth too much to abandon. Tor and Ketill found a buyer for them before the company moved on.

They gathered around their hoard of coin.

Cynewulf began. “Osric, you and Murdock should find us horses. You are the best judges of horseflesh. We’ll need one riding horse for each of us, and two draft horses. Get saddles, bridles, harness, and whatever else we need.”

Murdock looked confused. “Are ye thinking we need draft horses for pack saddles?”

Cynewulf smiled. “No. No pack saddles.”

Murdock raised his eyes. “Will we be strapping our goods onto their backs with nothing but their manes?”

Cynewulf let the smile turn into a smirk. “Let me worry about that.”

He turned to Tor and Ketill. “We’ll need provisions. Hold off on grain. Focus on dry goods and water barrels.”

Tor cocked his head. “You know something you’re not telling us.”

Cynewulf smiled sheepishly. “I don’t want to spoil the surprise,” was all he would say.

As promised, once the horses and provisions were paid for, Cynewulf handed the remaining purse to Tor. Murdock narrowed his eyes. He considered himself the better quartermaster of the two.

Cynewulf caught the look. “Don’t worry, Murdock. You’ll have somewhere to stow your crossbows.”

Osric handed the reins to Elswyth. She had never touched a horse, much less ridden one.

Elswyth stared up at the animal. “A horse? Perhaps I could walk?”

“Not if we mean to reach the farm first,” Osric said. He placed her hand against the mare’s neck. “Let her smell you. Then we’ll get you into the saddle.”

Cynewulf smiled at Elswyth. “Don’t worry. You may not have to ride for long.”

They rode out through the North Gate well into the afternoon. As they passed east of Charnwood, a line of sharp hills rose ahead of them. Cynewulf veered west, catching the company by surprise. No one complained... but several quizzical looks followed him.

He led them straight to the remains of the Dragon’s Teeth camp. Hidden beneath the brush was a fully intact wagon taken from one of Olaf’s intercepted supply trains out of York. The Dragon’s Teeth had no use for it and no spare horses to pull it.

The wagon held Cynewulf's share of the raiding loot: three small casks of ale and several large sacks of grain. Behind one sack of grain sat a belt with three straw-filled cups, each containing a plum-sized clay pot with a leather thong to lock each pot in place.

Cynewulf stepped back, eyes wide. The Dragons had left him a present... three of their prized Dragon’s Breath pots. He drew out the belt and turned one gently in his fingers, checking the wax seal. Then he inspected the others. His smile disappeared.

“Dragon’s Breath,” he said.

Osric looked at the little clay pots. “Did you say Dragon’s Breath?”

Cynewulf nodded. “These are the smell of evil.”

Osric studied the innocent brown clay, the wax, and the little cross.

“They don’t smell evil to me.”

Cynewulf's jaw clenched.

“If you had seen good warriors brought to their knees by what lies inside this pot, you would swear its fumes came from the Pit itself.”

Osric shrugged. "Then leave them here."

Cynewulf buckled the belt around his waist. “And let some child find them?”

He pulled his cloak over the belt. “No. They stay with me.”

Osric found spare axles and a spare wheel. He let out a long sigh of relief. Murdock began a thorough inspection.

Cynewulf turned his attention to the casks, and a smile grew across his face broader than any in the past ten years. “You should have seen the look on my captain’s face when each member claimed their share of the ale... three casks apiece, seventy-five in all. Lucky for us, the other seventy-two fit onto two wagons just like this one. Well... sixty-eight after the first night.”

One-Eye traced a finger over the casks. "So are we stopping here for the night? I have me _eye_ on this one."

Osric looked up from the wagon, satisfied it would hold together better than those of the fyrd. “We’re still behind, and this wagon will slow us down. Best we keep it light.”

One-Eye smiled. “Oh, I intend to lighten its load.”

Murdock harnessed the draft horses and tied his own riding horse behind the wagon. The company loaded their cumbersome gear into the bed, and there was still room to spare. Murdock climbed onto the driver’s bench, and they returned to the road.

He found a space for both crossbows underneath the bench.

Elswyth immediately claimed a space on the wagon. Her ride from the North Gate had proved that the best place for a horse was pulling any wagon she was in.

Cynewulf laughed. "Fine, we have two extra riding horses. We'll rotate them and spare their legs."

Elswyth sat near Murdock. She eyed a crossbow she could barely see poking out from beneath the bench.

“What do you call those?”

Murdock had been intent on avoiding the ruts in the drying mud. The question startled him.

“Those? They’re steel bows, lass. Only there’s a lever to loose the bolt.”

She thought of one of her stone-fall traps. “A trigger? Like in a rodent trap.”

“Very good, lass.”

“Would you teach me to shoot one?”

Murdock looked over his shoulder at her. “And why would ye be wanting to shoot my crossbow? Planning to hunt from the roadside? These bolts cost silver, and we’ll not waste them on oak trunks!”

Elswyth pressed earnestly. “Wouldn’t you rather ride with someone who knows how that works? If trouble finds us, your hands may be needed for the reins.”

Murdock had never taught a woman to use a weapon. He looked from Elswyth to the road ahead, then down toward the crossbow beneath the bench.

“Aye,” he said at last. “But ye’ll not loose a single bolt until I say. And don’t be thinking that makes ye a warrior-maiden... not until ye learn what each bolt costs in silver.”

He handed her the crossbow carefully, without a bolt, and explained the mechanism.

Before long, she had learned the rhythm of the draw and the timing of the trigger.

Murdock took the crossbow back and returned it beneath the bench. “We’ll practice against targets tomorrow morning. Sacks of grain only! Ye loose one past the sacks, and we’ll find it in the next town. And ye’ll be sewing those sacks up again when yer done.”

Murdock scratched his head.

“Funny. Ye can’t stand horses, but a string that can take your finger clean off is a novelty?”

She smiled. “I don’t trust things that think for themselves.”

_Especially when they wear red caps._

Her mind drifted back to that night, and to something Fussleton had said... that he would invite his family to soak their caps as well. It was something she needed to ask him about, if she could ever find the courage to draw the blade again.

She intended to learn all she could about avoiding another redcap. And if one found her, a crossbow seemed a reasonable thing to have... if only to disrupt its rhythm.

And Fussleton's title of _Spotted King_ filled her with a quiet dread.

Kings had courts. And she didn't know what kind of _court_ he kept.

She was determined to drive the thought from her mind.

Osric rode beside the wagon for a time, glancing often as far ahead as he could see.

“We cannot remain on this road all the way north,” he said.

Murdock looked over from the driver’s bench. “Aye lad. Cynewulf has the map. He'll know what to do. Ne'er you worry.”

Cynewulf overheard. "We'll determine our course on the morrow."

One-Eye laughed. “Unless their boots break before their axles do. Osric, did the fyrd ever ask you to mend a shoe with a wagon spoke?”

Murdock and Elswyth laughed.

“For now,” Osric replied. “But we need to pass them somewhere within the next six or seven days. We cannot follow the same road forever and hope they never notice us.”

Murdock sighed. "I'll be helping the pick of the road, lads. Lest we find this new wagon of ours in the ditch."

Osric looked back again.

“The greater danger is Galindo. Should we pass them before they recognize us, that is one thing. The men cannot catch us quickly. He can.”

Elswyth glanced toward him. “Because of his horse?”

Osric nodded. “That Iberian animal may be the fastest horse ever to eat Northumbrian oats.”

One-Eye frowned. “Is that a known measure of speed?”

“It is in the villages along the Tyne,” Osric said. “The children know Lucero better than they know some of their neighbors. Whenever Galindo rides through, they race beside him.”

“On horseback?” Elswyth asked.

“On foot.”

Tor laughed.

“He holds the horse to a trot,” Osric continued as he smiled. “They still lose.”

“Then why do they keep racing it?” Ketill asked.

“Because they are children.”

Murdock nodded gravely. “A dangerous condition. Most survive it.”

Osric narrowed his eyes, but decided to ignore him.

“If Galindo sees us ahead on the road, he can reach us before we find cover. Then he will ask why members of his baggage guard are traveling with fresh horses, a sound wagon, and enough provisions to make his train jealous.”

Tor looked toward the bed of the wagon. “And if he asks for them back?”

“He may not ask.”

One-Eye's voice grew dim. “Then we should make certain he never knows what he has lost. For his own sake.”

"Galindo's not my enemy," Osric replied. "I don't mean him harm."

“We travel the road tonight,” Cynewulf said. “Tomorrow we choose where to leave it. Somewhere in the next six or seven days, we must cross ahead of the fyrd without Galindo seeing us do it.”

They continued until the light faded and the road became a pale ribbon beneath the evening sky. Osric chose a low hollow beyond a stand of thorn, far enough from the road that neither wagon nor fire could easily be seen.

Murdock guided the draft horses down the slope and brought the wagon beneath a leaning oak. Tor and Ketill tended the animals. Elswyth gathered fallen branches from beneath the hedges, where the rain had not reached them. Cynewulf walked the edge of the hollow twice before allowing the fire to be lit.

## Chapter Twelve: Getting A Handle On Things

Osric fumbled in the dark, but managed to find a narrow set of stairs tucked into the corner of the forge. The room above had light streaming in through holes in the ceiling, and it was just a matter of time before he found an opening in a wall big enough to fit through. That's when he saw her.

Elswyth had an easier time finding an exit. The _frigidarium_ had a grand entranceway which led to the Jewry Wall. She emerged into the sunlight, shading her eyes which had grown accustomed to the dark.

They recognized each other from the night before. Each noticed the other was dressed in new attire. Each noticed the other was dragging a chest.

Each politely pretended not to.

It was easier than explaining where any of it had come from.

The pretense became considerably more difficult when it turned out they were both dragging chests filled with the belongings of dead _Ulfhednar_.

Each chest bore the same iron emblem riveted to its lid:

_An oak sprouting from a broken anvil, with two crossed hammers beneath._

Osric stopped. "No..."

He looked at her chest.

Then at his.

She looked at his. "No..."

Their chests hit the ground with heavy thuds.

"Where did you get that?" he demanded, pointing at the insignia on her coffer.

"Why? Did something... _weird_... happen to you too?"

Osric shook his head. "Understatement."

Elswyth shook her head. "Understatement."

Osric scanned her for injuries. "Did the wolf-men find you? I see you're still alive."

Elswyth sighed. "Barely alive. And you?"

"Barely alive. Well, were your Vikings... stripped... and... sorted?"

Elswyth's jaw dropped. "Sorted? What do you mean sorted?"

Osric opened his chest. The gear lay inside exactly as he had found it, already neatly folded. He presented the what he had found to Elswyth. He removed the top layer, which were a pair of Viking pants.

Elswyth frowned. "You... took the time to fold Viking pants... _neatly_?"

Osric dropped the pants back into the chest. "No!"

Elswyth's hands moved to her hips. "Well, then, who did?"

Osric slammed his chest shut. "You wouldn't believe me if I said another word."

"Well, I'm not opening mine. I didn't fold mine as neatly as you... or whoever... folded yours."

Osric closed his eyes. "I'll be fine if I never set eyes on Viking pants again."

"Complete agreement."

Osric opened his eyes and looked at their two boxes. "You know, we might move faster if I grab your handle..."

"And I grab your other handle..."

They found themselves standing one in front, one in back. Elswyth's coffer was in their right hands, and Osric's in their left.

Elswyth sighed. "Great, I'm in front. But where are we going?"

"I don't know. Anywhere but here?"

"Well, I can't go home... yet."

"Why not?"

Elswyth shook her head. "You wouldn't believe me."

"Well I can't go home, ever."

"Why not?"

Osric closed his eyes once more. "Look, can we just agree that we avoid questions for now? We just need to find a place to... think."

"Well if you need a priest, the church is just a few steps that way."

"I'm new here. Anything's fine."

"You know, the Danes own the town again."

Osric looked around for anyone watching them. No one was. "Oh. We probably shouldn't walk through town with wolf-men loot in our chests."

Elswyth nodded. "Might look... bad."

"Very bad."

Elswyth turned her head back to the frigidarium. "I spent the night in the room with the freezing pool. There's nobody there."

"Was there a place to hide two chests?"

"Yes. The place where you came from, was there a place to hide three more bodies?"

"There was a forge."

A smile crept onto Elswyth's face. "Are you thinking what I'm thinking?"

"Not sure. Can't see your face."

"If there's fuel for that forge..."

"Nooo. That doesn't feel right..."

"They're going to start to smell."

"You want to burn them?"

Elswyth's eyes narrowed. "_They tried to kill us_."

She turned, leading Osric back to the ruin of the bathhouse. "Think of it as a funeral pyre."

Osric opened his mouth to object, then found he had none.

Instead, another thought took hold.

Ealhild.

The children.

They still lay beneath the snow by the river Tyne... if they lay there at all. If the hearth in Alfheimr had not been the whole truth, the thaw would soon uncover them. If it had...

Osric tried to quicken the pace from behind Elswyth, pushing her forward. "We need to hurry."

"Hurry?"

"I need to get home."

"You said you had no home."

"My kin. They must be buried."

"Slow down!"

"And I'll need a horse."

Elswyth guided them to the rainwater chute and they slid in with their chests. They shed their armor before getting into the water. "Be mindful of the pool. The water is..."

Osric waded in. "Freezing!"

"Usually I get only one bath for Easter! I've had two baths in two days."

Osric laughed. "Life as a charcoaler?"

"Black as a soot-stained raven."

They hid the chests beneath broken stone and shattered tile before retrieving the three bodies from the bathhouse. Once the corpses had been lowered through the chute into the abandoned forge, they dressed again in their new armor and descended after them.

Elswyth marveled at the engineering of the forge. She had delivered charcoal to forges before, but most of them had been cobbled stone hearths, a ramshackle roof, and a bellows whose leather was barely holding together.

She traced her fingers on the brick lines. "This doesn't look like... what I expected."

Her eyes fell upon the dead bodies, and the pile of charcoal beneath them. "Did you see this?"

Osric had his hands on a dead Viking and started to pull it off the heap. "The charcoal?"

"This is _my coal_. I cut it to these lengths."

Osric stopped pulling and looked. "The forge was lit yesterday. Suddenly... _lit_. Like someone else was here."

Elswyth stopped and stared at Osric. "Do _not_ blame this on the _Coaler-Wight_."

Osric blinked. "...the what?"

Elswyth thought for a long moment. She didn't want to ask this, but now she felt she had no choice.

Elswyth cleared her throat. "I'm going to ask you something, and don't laugh."

"I'm well beyond laughing. But you may ask."

"Did you find this smithy by yourself in the dark, or were you... _led_... here?"

Osric composed himself before responding. He knew this would sound ridiculous. "By a giggling child?"

Elswyth reeled backward. "NOO!"

"Does that mean something to you?"

Elswyth stared at the charcoal.

She thought of the giggling children.

The mushroom nets.

The redcap.

The forge.

The forge-elves.

Coal at the edges of her pit. This coal.

They hadn't followed her.

They had been here all along.

"We have to leave. Now!"

Osric looked back down at the body in front of him. "The bodies?"

"Never mind them. Those little... _giggling things_... will take care of them. We don't want to be here. Believe me."

"What? What's going on?"

"Our... _gifts_? I think I know who gave them to us."

"Who?"

Elswyth whispered, "The people who live here."

Osric whispered, "_Nobody_ lives here."

Elswyth looked around, whispering quieter still, "Yes... _nobodies_... _NO... BODIES..._ _who giggle like children._"

Osric looked at her for a long moment.

Osric dropped the body in his hands and escaped through the narrow passageways. Elswyth was right behind him.

Neither looked back as they ran to the church.

Elswyth and Osric entered the Old Frith.

The churchyard had become a refuge for every sort of soul. Farmers, merchants, craftsmen, widows, and travelers wandered among the graves and parish buildings, waiting for news, bread and broth, or simply somewhere safe to stand.

Rumors drifted through the crowd as freely as the smoke. Last night, there was a battle outside the North Gate. Some insisted the peace had been broken. Others swore it had held. The Danes now holding the town seemed far more interested in making silver from the wagons themselves than in settling the argument.

Regardless, nobody paid them any heed.

The Danes were occupied with restoring order in the streets and had little interest in the frightened crowds sheltering in the churchyard. So long as the clergy kept them peaceful, they were the Church's concern, not the soldiers'.

Osric immediately spotted Murdock by his red braids. He was gathered with what remained of the fyrd's baggage train beside one of the surviving stone piers of the Shadow Gate. Heavy oak timbers had been passed through holes bored in the old Roman masonry to brace the repaired breach. One of the great beams now served as an impromptu bench.

Murdock saw them approach and nearly dropped his bowl of broth.

"By the bells of the heavenly gates... what have we here?"

He stood and turned his head to the side.

"Osric, as I stand here... I barely recognized you. We thought you two were dead."

His attention turned to his new armor and attire, the threads still glistening in the sun. He put his hand on Osric's new wool cloak to feel the material.

"These haven't seen a single day in the mud. Good thing we don't have another axle for you to fix."

A smile crept across Murdock's face.

"Or shall I track down one fer ye so we can say we broke 'em in?"

A look of terror swept over Osric's face.

Murdock burst out laughing.

"Och, would ye look at his face!"

He clapped Osric on the shoulder.

"Easy, lad. I'm not baggage master anymore. As ye can see, there's precious little baggage left to master."

Murdock spread his hands toward the handful of sacks, pots, pans, and odds and ends that were all that remained of the train.

He shrugged.

"If you're still a thrall to the fyrd, that's between you, Galindo, and God. But..."

He gave Osric a proper head-to-toe accounting.

"I'd wager an ox to a goose egg that you're the best-dressed thrall in all of Britain."

He turned his attention to Elswyth.

"And you... what did the two of ye do, get chased by Vikings that fancied themselves tailors?"

Elswyth cleared her throat.

"Well it's strange that..."

Murdock held up his hand, shaking his head.

"Actually, save it lass. I've already heard about naked giants and angels handing out armor."

He jabbed a thumb towards Cynewulf's black-oiled mail.

"If you've got another perfectly true tale that'll sound like drunken nonsense tomorrow, I'd rather hear it after I find some ale."

Osric and Elswyth looked at each other, then at Cynewulf. His skin-cracked hand dropped onto the handle of _Wodbora_. He quickly drew his cloak over it. His face curled into a slight smirk, and his eyes veered downward, staring at the the drying mud.

One-Eye leaned in. A smile crept across his face.

"So, if the angels' price for new kit is a miracle, does dropping a man at fifty paces count? I mean, they must know I have only one eye?"

The group chuckled, but One-Eye received no answer. He seemed genuinely disappointed.

They sat together a while longer, the day at last feeling like spring had made good on its promise.

Osric stood before them, his hands clasped behind his back. What came next felt like a momentous ask.

"I have urgent business in _Hagustealdsham_. I need a horse."

Murdock's mouth stood agape.

"For the blood of the saints, why boy? Ye want to get _back_ to the fyrd?"

"I can understand if none of you wish to go with me, but I have unfinished business."

Elswyth nudged Osric with her elbow.

"How far is it... to your farm?"

"Two weeks on foot, and the fyrd left yesterday. Six days comfortably, if everyone has horses."

Since arriving in Leicester with her parents as a young girl, Elswyth had been no farther than the Charnwood Forest and Leicester. As someone who had spent years pretending to be a ghost, one could almost say she had never truly belonged to either.

For the first time, she felt like a sapling in a pot—not bound to the earth.

"Well, what are we waiting for?"

Cynewulf knew this conversation well in his own mind. His own _Liturgy of the Road_ had drilled it into him from the day he left Leicester long ago.

"Provisions. Water..."

Tor interrupted.

"I think those are my concerns. I'm the quartermaster. I say two draft horses, each with..."

Murdock chimed in.

"Nobody is going to manage this company's baggage... but me! Now how many are going?"

Cynewulf stood back and smiled, as he watched Murdock and Tor argue about priorities. He watched as Osric and Elswyth talked about the wolf-men's loot, and how that could be sold quietly by Tor to the blacksmiths and the thread-makers. He watched Elswyth laugh as One-Eye teased her about missing a spot of soot in her hair.

The oddly-clothed Viking sat down next to Cynewulf.

"Group. Good."

Cynewulf smiled.

Then it struck him.

He had never asked the man's name.

Nor the name of the companion who had died beside him the night before.

"I didn't catch your name."

"Name Ketill."

"You don't look Dane. You don't sound Dane. Where are you from?"

"Aye. From where sunrise meet ice. Do you know Volga? River in east."

Cynewulf had studied a map of the known world when he was a student. He remembered it as being on the eastern edge of the world.

"Yes, I've heard of it."

"Find Volga then keep going."

Cynewulf quietly marveled at the journey this man had taken to get here.

"You're far from home. I can't even think of how long that must have taken."

"Journey not far."

Ketill looked at his boots, caked with mud, and smiled.

"Not far... if home always under feet."

"And your companion's name? Where was he from?"

"His name is _drengr_. His home is here."

He pointed to his chest.

Cynewulf was confused. He knew _drengr_ to mean a companion, an honorable man, a sworn brother.

"What did his mother call him?"

Ketill smiled.

"Name not important. Face everything."

Ketill looked to the horizon.

"He had... __Noble__... face."

Ketill looked back at Cynewulf.

"And now he where he thinks he should be."

Cynewulf thought back to the _witan-sleep_ of the ravens. His promise to honor the Viking boy whose life he took without a second thought. The boy's name was _Penance_. Ketill had done the same for his oath-sworn, and that name was _Noble_.

Cynewulf realized that people were more than souls carried by the Road, by the wind, or by the will of gods. Before all of that, they were remembered. They were loved. They were sons and daughters, brothers and sisters, parents and oath-sworn. Their histories lived on in memory. Their deeds endured in story.

Someday, others would tell his.

He wanted to be worthy of it.

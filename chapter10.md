## Chapter 10: New Dawn

Woden begins the book nervous about the future. Nervous about Ragnarok.

Not easy to predict.

Not perfectly predictable.

But predictable in principle.

Why else gather information?

Why else sacrifice an eye?

Why else maintain ravens?

Why else interrogate prophecy?

Why else build Valhalla?

Every action assumes:

> If I know enough variables, I can understand the future. Maybe not to undo the future, but to face it as prepared as I can.

---

The experiment destroys that assumption.

Not because free will wins.

Not because wyrd dies.

Because the number of variables is effectively infinite.

---

Think about Osric.

Why does he eventually choose what he chooses?

Woden's experiment says:

- Blade.
- Cost.
- Wyrd.

Three variables.

Simple.

Clean.

---

Freyr starts listing:

- Ealhild.
- Her father.
- The cows.
- The threshold stone.
- Gerðr's Tears.
- The Tyne Valley.
- The children.
- The sheep-trading neighbor.
- The memory of grief.
- The harvest.
- The weather.
- The battle.
- The stories he heard as a child.

And suddenly Osric's decision becomes inseparable from his life.

---

The realization isn't:

> "Wyrd is false."

It's:

> "No event has a single cause."

That's a huge difference.

---

Imagine Woden standing before the results.

Trying to isolate the decisive variable.

The one thing that explains the outcome.

The scientific impulse.

The need for causality.

---

And Freyr simply says:

> "Which root fed the tree?"

Woden:

> "All of them."

Freyr:

> "Exactly."

---

Now we're in Freyr's domain.

Because farmers understand emergence.

A harvest is never:

- Rain.
- Soil.
- Seed.

It's all of them together.

You cannot separate them.

---

Which means Woden's revelation is not:

> Nothing can be predicted.

That's actually a little too strong.

Farmers predict all the time.

Sailors predict.

Hunters predict.

Gods predict.

---

The revelation is:

> Nothing can be predicted completely.

And that's far more profound.

---

Because it means the Norns may still be right.

Ragnarök may still happen.

Wyrd may still exist.

But certainty becomes impossible.

---

That's the true horror for Woden.

Not uncertainty.

**Irreducibility.**

The realization that reality cannot be simplified into a tidy model.

No matter how much knowledge he accumulates.

No matter how many ravens he sends.

No matter how many runes he learns.

---

And that's when Freyr's sacrifice finally clicks.

Because Freyr never needed certainty.

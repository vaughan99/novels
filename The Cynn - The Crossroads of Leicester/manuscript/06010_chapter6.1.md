## Part One: The Liturgy of the Bell

_A bell rings in the distance, and people orient themselves to it. They use it to judge time, and to mark history. They use it to call the faithful to confession, to declare peace, and to announce marriage. When a man makes bells, he is giving voice to time. But no one tells the bell-maker when the bell will toll for him._

_These are the lessons of the Bell._

This story began earlier, in 913 AD. It was Shrove Tuesday, the week before Lent, a day for confessions in the Church of Augustine and Columba. The Shrove-Bells rang throughout Leicester.

Cynewulf was not born to the sword. He was born to carpenters, and had a fairly boring life. As a boy of ten winters, he knelt in the nave at his first confession, surrounded by villagers.

These were the old days of the confessional, when the priest sat openly among the congregation, one hand on the penitent's shoulder, his stole draped over the penitent and a penitential in his other hand. The confession was made openly, witnessed by all.

Cynewulf's father stood behind him, his eyes bent somberly at his child confessing before the seated priest.

"Father, my name is Cynewulf... son of Godric. There is a heat in me," he whispered. "I looked at my best friend Ordgar and... I wished him into the fire. We were picking through the iron bits meant for my father's small nail-forge, and he found a garnet. I'm told such things are rare. Garnets are swallowed by smelt-fire. I was jealous of the prized bauble he had found, and I wanted it for myself. And then the forge-spark jumped. I watched him burn, and for a heartbeat, I did not move."

His friend was not terribly injured that day, but accusations were made, and they were no longer friends.

"Cynewulf, that is an ember-hate. You must watch it carefully, and see that it dies quickly," the priest said.

He released his grip on Cynewulf's shoulder and consulted the penitential, a book of soul-tariffs.

The book snapped closed.

"Cynewulf, you shall serve the bell-pits of the church from now through Lent. You will know the bite of embers on your skin and the glow-heat of the castings. Do this, and your sin shall be forgiven. Go forth, report this to your father, and steel yourself to the bell-master."

The confession that day brought him to the church.

The work did not seem like work.

He spent Lent pressing clay for molds, shoveling charcoal, setting the burn, and standing back as bronze was poured. The heat felt cleansing.

He also saw the work of the priesthood itself, the deference they received and the catharsis they delivered.

It felt like a calling.

When his penance was served, Cynewulf told his father he wanted the bell-pits and then the priesthood. The bell-master had space for a new apprentice. The church had space for an acolyte-to-come. His labor would help pay for Latin lessons and the tutelage he required.

Godric was proud. It was a noble beginning for his son, an apprenticeship of trade and a life of humble privilege. The house had been quiet since Cynewulf's mother's passing, and Godric seemed to find a new, reflected light in his son's sudden, burning ambition.

When the Lent season ended, Godric carried his son's belongings through the church gate himself. It was a short walk from the cottage, yet it felt strangely longer than any road he had ever traveled.

They embraced awkwardly, each pretending the distance was nothing.

Without ceremony, the Church became Cynewulf's household.

The priests became his tutors, the bell-master his taskmaster, and the older acolytes his brothers. Bells woke him, summoned him to prayer, called him to meals, and finally sent him to sleep. Their voices measured not only the hours of Leicester, but the years of his youth.

He rose before dawn, hauled charcoal, mixed clay, swept ash, and learned to coax liquid bronze from stubborn metal. The bell-master was demanding but fair. Every mistake had a lesson.

Cynewulf liked the certainty.

Bronze did not flatter.

Bells did not lie.

A mold was either true or it was not.

When the castings were finished, another kind of labor began.

Godric arrived with chisels, augers, adzes, and oak timbers. He was no founder, but the bell was only half complete until it had a frame worthy of its voice. Together father and son fitted beams, carved joints, and hoisted bronze into new towers.

Cynewulf learned that a bell could be perfectly cast and still ring poorly if mounted by careless hands.

His father's craft gave the bell its freedom to speak.

It became their shared labor. Godric rarely praised him aloud, but his trust grew quietly. Before long he handed Cynewulf measurements instead of instructions. They solved problems together, debating joints and braces over scraps of charcoal and rough planks.

The priests noticed.

So did visiting clerics.

Word of Godric's craftsmanship passed from one church to another. Soon he was being called beyond Leicester to repair old frames and raise new ones.

At first his journeys lasted days.

Then weeks.

He traveled with priests, craftsmen, merchants, or pilgrims whenever he could, and when he returned, he brought stories.

Towering churches.

Crowded markets.

Strange accents.

Distant roads.

Sometimes he laid a small trinket or unfamiliar coin before Cynewulf, but the stories were always the greater gift.

Godric kept the harsher truths of the Road to himself.

He seldom spoke of flooded crossings, spoiled provisions, fever, highwaymen, or nights spent listening for footsteps beyond the firelight.

Those were burdens for grown men, he believed, not stories to place upon the shoulders of a boy who still looked toward the horizon with wonder.

So Cynewulf inherited his father's love of the Road, but not his caution.

Each journey ended the same way. Godric unpacked his tools. He rested. Then another messenger arrived.

Cynewulf helped load the wagon each time.

He was proud. He was also left behind.

The Church itself became his window onto the world. Travelers, merchants, pilgrims, reeves, priests, and scholars passed beneath its roof. As his Latin improved, Cynewulf copied letters, translated names, and listened to tales of places he had never seen.

The confessional offered still more. People confessed more than sins. Each confession painted another Road he had never walked.

His tutors praised his memory. Latin came easily. Scripture followed. Villagers who had known him as a charcoal-blackened apprentice now bowed their heads as he read from books they themselves could not understand.

The change fascinated him. The words had not changed. Only the speaker had. He noticed how silence fell when Latin filled the nave. Men who argued endlessly with one another became quiet before a language none of them spoke.

Knowledge carried weight. Some knowledge carried more.

Years passed.

The bell-master entrusted Cynewulf with greater responsibility. He judged bronze by sound, inspected molds, and supervised younger boys entering the pits as he once had.

Whenever a bell rang true, he felt satisfaction knowing how many unseen hands had contributed to one clear note.

Yet it was rarely those hands people remembered. The priest was praised. The donor was thanked. The founder signed the casting.

The apprentices were forgotten.

Cynewulf did not complain. He simply noticed.

Godric seemed content with such things. "Let the bell remember us," he once said while packing his tools for another journey. "People forget craftsmen. Good work doesn't."

Cynewulf nodded.

He was not certain he agreed.

As Godric's reputation grew, his absences lengthened. Father and son still worked well whenever their paths crossed, but increasingly they resembled fellow craftsmen sharing a workshop rather than family sharing a household.

Cynewulf was proud of him.

Usually. When a bishop praised Godric's ingenuity, warmth came first. Then a tiny ember of resentment glowed beneath Cynewulf's ribs before he smothered it in shame.

The praise belonged to Godric.

He knew that.

Why, then, did the ember refuse to die?

And the Road seemed always to pass through Leicester without stopping for him. Cynewulf wondered what lay beyond the next hill. Surely every road eventually led to another church. And every church had use for another priest.

Years passed, and his apprenticeship came to its proper end. Before the altar he knelt, received the laying on of hands, and rose a deacon.

The work changed little. He still swept the nave, prepared the altar, and tended the bells.

Yet something had changed all the same. He was no longer merely the master's hands. He had become one of the bell-masters himself.

The first serious quarrel came not over prayer, but over bronze. A younger workman reached for a mold that had sat out on a rainy night.

Molten bronze explodes wet molds.

Cynewulf caught his wrist and shoved him backward so hard the man stumbled into another apprentice. Harsh words followed. One accusation became another until both men stood red-faced beside the crucible, shouting over the roar of the furnace.

Godric was no longer there to parent him. He had sold the family cottage to buy passage through Frankia and beyond, carrying his craft all the way toward Rome.

The old priest stepped between the two men.

"Enough."

His voice was quiet. For a long moment he looked only at Cynewulf. "I have watched men tend hearths my whole life. A good fire warms a house. A bad one devours it."

Cynewulf lowered his eyes.

"The flame in you burns hot, my son. There is great warmth in it. But I fear there is also a little coal that never cools."

The words lingered long after the bronze hardened.

Cynewulf remembered the warning. He did not yet understand it.

A few weeks before Shrove Tuesday arrived that year, he took his vows and was ordained a priest.

When the Shrove-Bells rang once more, Cynewulf sat in the same seat from which a priest had once heard him confess his own ember-hate.

Now he heard the sins of others.

He kept the small, dark sins of his own heart tucked away in the back of his mind, convincing himself that he was the perfect guide. He knew the weight of sin.

Therefore, surely, he knew how to lead others toward absolution.

The village folk came one by one. Hours passed.

At the back of the church lingered a tall, silent figure.

He was the final penitent.

An elderly man approached in a simple grey robe with a walking staff and a slow, steady gait. Most of the villagers had already gone. His staff echoed against the stone as he approached.

He stood before Cynewulf as though taking his measure.

Then the old man drew back his cowl and knelt.

Long grey hair fell across one side of his weathered face.

Cynewulf's eyes settled on the loop of ash branches hanging from his neck. The man had made his own cross from gnarled wood, perhaps from the same tree as his staff. The branches were bound with fresh undyed wool.

He smelled of sheep's lanolin and the night air.

Cynewulf laid his hand upon the man's shoulder.

"Let us begin. Tell me your name."

The old man replied in a thick Danish accent.

"I... have no name of remembrance. My name died with my old life."

"God knows your name, dear shepherd. I need not hear it. Tell me the sins of an old shepherd, so that I may guide you."

The shepherd leaned back and raised his voice.

"I've been a shepherd all my life. I have seen grass grow green and go to stubble more times than stars in the sky. In the past, I knew my flock. I knew when the ewe would have twins. I knew when the ram would break the gate.

"I trained dogs to protect my sheep. I sent the dogs into new pastures to claim them for my flock. For a time, the dogs did well, and my sheep prospered.

"Down in the valley, there is a new Master. He has built stone walls to keep his sheep in. He has trained his sheep to protect themselves. My sheep see my neighbor's walls, and they are high. They leave to seek comfort inside them.

"Now the dogs have forgotten the field they are in.

"They prey on all the sheep."

The old shepherd leaned his forehead against his staff.

"My sin is not the spilt blood of sheep. My sin is that I'm abandoning my dogs, Father. My sheep I am leaving to the farmer-in-the-meadow, should he want them. May God have pity on my soul."

The heavy, weeping weight vanished from him in a heartbeat.

His head rose.

"There is a final curse I must mention. I saw it in the night. A flight of both light and dark in the sky, of black wings and spectral clouds. It sounded as wolf howl and rattling bone. It was a Hate... a Wodhere... from the Old Times."

The hairs on Cynewulf's neck rose.

"Describe this Hate, please."

"I've said too much. It was not meant for the living."

Cynewulf thought hard.

_What could I possibly prescribe for such a man?_

As he reached for his penitential, the shepherd leaned close.

With one final, smiling breath, he spoke in perfect Latin:

"Memento mei bene."

Cynewulf froze.

_Remember me well?_

He snapped his eyes upward.

The grey shepherd was already halfway out the door.

Cynewulf couldn't remember hearing the knock of the staff or the shuffle of his retreat.

That last confession struck him as odd.

_Trained dogs? Who was this shepherd? How did he know Latin when he could barely speak English?_

He found an answer quickly enough.

_He probably overheard priests speaking it. Perhaps he obsessed over the phrase while tending sheep until he perfected the pronunciation._

_It's nothing._

Cynewulf tried to put the confession out of mind. It stayed with him for the rest of his days.

_Bells may ring. Whether you hear them depends on where you stand, and whether you choose to listen. A bell may toll directly before you, loud enough to shatter the air, but if you decide not to hear? You might as well be deaf._

_So sayeth the Bell._

On March 14th, 933 AD, the black day of Leicester's village-folk arrived.

The Ulfhednar struck Leicester.

It was the day Elswyth fled into the Charnwood.

Cynewulf was in his small priest-home, cataloging donations and teaching Latin to three students when the church erupted in noise.

He barred the door.

He sent the students into a closet.

Among the morning's donations lay a byrnie once owned by a retired thegn. Cynewulf pulled it over his priestly woolens. Then he wrapped a spare censer-chain around his fist.

He would not let the night fall upon him and his flock without a fight.

His breathless prayers competed with the uproar until something struck the door.

A shoulder.

Then a boot.

Again.

The door gave way.

A young Viking stood in the opening, a freshly cropped wolf upon his head. He howled and leveled his axe toward Cynewulf's chest.

"Priest-blood... for the wolf!"

The boy looked even more scared than Cynewulf felt.

Cynewulf's ember-hate erupted.

_How dare these creatures invade our sanctum?_

He hurled himself into the young Ulfhedinn. The Viking had no time to raise his shield. They hit the floor.

Cynewulf swung the chain-fist into the boy's jaw. There was a sickening crack. He struck again.

The deed was final.

Cynewulf stood over the broken jaw, twisted neck, and spreading blood.

Laying next to the boy were his axe and shield.

Beyond the doorway lay a small chest of loot.

Cynewulf looked back toward his own table. The donated silver plates and goblets gleamed there with a sack that had been used to carry them.

It all appeared as one to him: a soldier's kit, a traveler's purse, and a bag of loot.

And beyond the broken doorway, a town too frightened to notice one priest disappearing.

For years the Road had passed through Leicester without stopping for him. Now it stood open.

Cynewulf told the children to take their opportunity.

"Run!"

Once they were gone, he took everything else.

He left Leicester bent beneath the weight of silver and steel. Pawning church plate within the city would be scorn-folly; his new life would have to begin elsewhere.

In time, perhaps Leicester would forget his name.

Perhaps they would think him only another lamb sacrificed to the wolves.

Behind him, the bells called out to warn the town. Many times, he had been the one pulling the rope to let loose the peal. That life was no longer his.

_The bells, large and small, announce the turning points in a life: Birth, Baptism, Communion, Marriage, Death. But there is no bell sound for the abandonment of a vocation or a life. There is no warning when the choice is irreversible. Bells do not toll for the unwise._

_So sayeth the Bell._

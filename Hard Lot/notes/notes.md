# HARD LOT
## Canonical Worldbuilding Notes — Teller's Gorge
**Version 2.2 | October 2026**

**Project:** *Hard Lot*  
**Genre:** Revisionist Western / Supernatural Mystery / Philosophical Fiction  
**Primary setting:** Teller's Gorge, a fictional Colorado silver-mining town  
**Likely period:** Late 1870s–1880s; exact year undecided  
**Status:** Working canon with several unresolved plot details

---

## 1. The Novel's Core Premise

*Hard Lot* follows Sol Salazar, a traveling salt salesman, and his unnamed business partner as they arrive in Teller's Gorge, Colorado.

The book initially appears to be a morally complicated Western about a merchant who becomes entangled in the corruption of a mining town.

The deeper premise, revealed toward the conclusion, is that Sol is the biblical Lot and his partner is one of the angels who rescued him from Sodom.

The angel selects towns and historical circumstances for Lot to confront. Lot determines how to respond to the wrongdoing he encounters.

The arrangement is an ongoing angel-led moral examination inspired by Abraham's argument with God over Sodom in Genesis 18.

The angel evaluates Lot while also studying human behavior through him.

Lot already understands that he is being examined. He has accepted the arrangement and sincerely tries to make the best decisions he can.

The reader initially does not understand the true nature of their relationship.

**The fundamental question is whether the righteous can be protected when they depend upon a system that is genuinely wicked.**

---

## 2. Teller's Gorge — The Setting

Teller's Gorge is a fictional silver-mining community in the mountains of Colorado.

Its primary economic activity is extracting and processing silver ore.

The town is dominated by a powerful mining company that owns or controls most of the infrastructure essential to everyday life.

Its interests include:

- The silver mine and associated reduction mill.
- The company general store.
- Much of the local housing.
- Water collection, pumping, and distribution.
- Important transport and commercial infrastructure.
- Employment, wages, company credit, and potentially local lending arrangements.

The company provides genuine economic benefits, including employment, infrastructure, and access to essential goods.

However, these benefits have become instruments of economic coercion.

The town's prosperity and its exploitation arise from the same system.

The mining company should not be portrayed as an organization that produces nothing of value. Its contributions to the town are real, and its abuses are equally real.

---

## 3. Silver Refining and the Salt Trade

The decision to make Teller's Gorge a silver-mining town is central to the setting.

Some nineteenth-century silver-processing methods used ordinary salt as a reagent, particularly in chloridizing roasting and associated extraction processes.

The mine's reduction mill employs a historically appropriate salt-consuming process.

As a result, salt is an industrial necessity, not merely a household commodity.

Sol arrives with commercial quantities of salt for which the mill is a major potential customer.

His other potential customers include butchers, general merchants, boardinghouses, ranchers, hotels, and households.

This gives him a credible reason to meet people across different social and economic classes.

### Historical constraints

Not every Colorado silver ore was processed using salt. The mill's technology, ore composition, and extraction method must be chosen consistently.

A plausible approach is a mill using chloridizing roasting followed by an appropriate silver-extraction process, but exact technical details remain subject to research.

The setting should be historically credible rather than relying on generalized imagery of a mining town.

---

## 4. The Company Scrip Economy

A central feature of Teller's Gorge is its dependence on company-issued scrip or credit.

Workers receive some or much of their compensation through a system tied to the mining company.

The company store accepts this credit for food, clothing, tools, and household necessities.

Company housing, water, supplies, and other expenses may be charged against workers' accounts.

This creates a powerful economic dependency.

Workers may nominally earn wages while struggling to accumulate cash that can be spent outside the community.

Debt can make leaving Teller's Gorge extremely difficult.

### Important nuance

The scrip arrangement should not be treated as inherently identical to slavery. Its coercive features depend upon the specific rules imposed by the company.

The town's system is deliberately exploitative, but the precise wage arrangements, redemption rules, debt terms, and enforcement methods remain to be developed.

The company may occasionally pay cash or redeem credit at unfavorable terms.

Historically, company stores and scrip were especially prominent in some mining communities, including coal camps. The precise application to this fictional Colorado silver town requires careful period research.

---

## 5. The Exploitation of Children

Child labor and possible debt bondage are contemplated as significant elements of the town's wickedness.

Families unable to repay company debts may be pressured into providing their children's labor.

The company can defend this practice as legitimate employment, apprenticeship, or household debt repayment.

However, the children's ability to refuse may be severely constrained.

Possible employment includes mine-related support work, ore handling, sorting, transportation, company services, or other age- and period-appropriate jobs.

The precise occupations and enforcement arrangements remain undecided.

The intended moral problem is that parents may be compelled to expose their children to dangerous labor simply to keep their households supplied and housed.

Some adults may rationalize the arrangement as necessary. Others may exploit it for personal benefit.

Not every participating parent is equally culpable.

**The town's survival is partly sustained by transferring economic suffering onto its children.**

This creates a direct thematic connection to Lot's conduct in Genesis 19, when he offered his daughters to the mob threatening his guests.

---

## 6. The Righteous Within a Wicked System

Teller's Gorge contains people who recognize the company's abuses and actively demand reform.

They are not simply passive victims.

Some may organize workers, defend children, expose dangerous practices, or demand fair compensation.

Crucially, these people also depend upon the company.

They need its employment, housing, water, and commercial infrastructure.

If the company collapses, they may suffer more immediately than its wealthy owners.

Possible characters include a labor organizer, a widowed parent, a skilled mill mechanic, a doctor, or a community member advocating for children's welfare.

These are candidate roles, not finalized characters.

Their existence gives concrete form to Abraham's question:

**Should the righteous suffer alongside the wicked?**

The righteous are not necessarily morally flawless. They may have made compromises to survive.

Their imperfections do not erase the moral significance of their efforts to resist wrongdoing.

---

## 7. The Mine Owner and the Nature of Wickedness

The primary company owner or controlling figure should possess a defensible public history.

He may have invested heavily in Teller's Gorge, developed the silver deposits, constructed infrastructure, attracted workers, and helped establish the town.

He may sincerely believe that his company is indispensable.

His argument could be that dismantling his operation would destroy the livelihoods of the very people who condemn him.

That argument may contain considerable truth.

However, the company uses its economic power to maintain exploitative labor conditions and prevent meaningful independence.

The antagonist need not be hypocritical about every aspect of his conduct.

He may genuinely consider himself a builder and benefactor.

This creates an essential distinction:

**A person can accomplish real good while also committing serious wrongdoing.**

Lot must determine whether the good changes what justice demands of the wrongdoer.

---

## 8. Sol Salazar's Commercial Role

Sol is the outward-facing salesman in the partnership.

His unnamed partner chooses the destination, ostensibly because he has identified a promising market for salt.

The two present as equal business partners.

Sol handles customers and cultivates relationships. The partner handles strategic decisions about travel and commercial opportunities.

Sol's visits to the mill, company store, households, and independent businesses gradually reveal Teller's Gorge's economic structure.

He is not a detective who arrives intending to investigate corruption.

His discoveries arise naturally from conducting legitimate business.

A particularly useful commercial conflict involves the distinction between company scrip and ordinary money.

Sol may be offered company credit in payment for salt, even though such credit cannot easily be spent or redeemed elsewhere.

Because he is an outsider, he can question arrangements that local residents have come to regard as normal.

However, Sol also has his own weakness.

He sometimes confuses social perception, status, and accepted custom with moral righteousness.

The town repeatedly challenges that error.

His preferred weapon, if violence becomes necessary, is a shotgun rather than a conventional gunslinger's revolver. The exact weapon depends on the selected year.

---

## 9. The Central Moral Dilemma

Lot encounters a town whose dominant institution is genuinely wicked but also genuinely necessary to its inhabitants.

He must decide what response justice requires.

Removing the company owner may not remove the economic system.

Destroying the company may end exploitation while depriving families of essential resources.

Sparing the company's leadership may preserve employment while allowing abuses to continue.

Workers may demand justice while pleading with Sol not to dismantle the infrastructure upon which their children depend.

There may be opportunities for reform, restitution, changes in ownership, or other responses beyond execution and inaction.

The specific alternatives must arise credibly from the town's circumstances.

The novel should not construct an artificially impossible dilemma merely to force a tragic ending.

Lot's choices should have real consequences, including the possibility that he overlooks a better solution.

**The central test is whether Lot can recognize and oppose evil without sacrificing the people he means to protect.**

---

## 10. The Angel's Role

The unnamed angel is genuinely one of the beings who led Lot from Sodom.

He is neither Satan nor a disguised demon.

He selects Teller's Gorge as the destination, apparently because its mine offers profitable opportunities for salt sales.

In reality, the town presents a significant moral examination.

The angel does not simply give Sol a list of villains.

Sol discovers the town's condition through his own commercial and social encounters.

The angel discusses Sol's observations and decisions, often in the manner of an ordinary business partner.

Their relationship is familiar, affectionate, occasionally teasing, and intellectually reciprocal.

Lot already knows the true nature of his companion.

The angel is evaluating Lot but may also be trying to understand human moral behavior.

His curiosity about Abraham's argument is central to their relationship.

The angel heard a persuasive abstract argument about justice and has chosen to explore what that argument means when applied to actual human lives.

**The angel selects the circumstances. Lot chooses how the story ends.**

---

## 11. Narrative Misdirection and Identity

The novel's structure draws upon several narrative influences:

| Work | Influence |
|---|---|
| *Unforgiven* | Moral complexity of violence, culpability, and redemption |
| *High Plains Drifter* | Supernatural judgment in a Western setting |
| *True Grit* | Determination, endurance, and moral certainty |
| *Quantum Leap* | New identities and historical circumstances serving a recurring moral inquiry |
| *Shutter Island* | A late revelation that changes the meaning of the protagonist's apparent mission |

Sol already understands more about his situation than the reader does.

His assigned historical identity includes the practical language abilities needed to operate in his environment.

He may occasionally ask about the social or moral meaning of a word or custom, not because he cannot speak the language, but because linguistic fluency does not confer complete cultural understanding.

Such conversations should initially appear to be ordinary exchanges between traveling merchants.

Only later does the reader recognize their underlying significance.

---

## 12. The Canonical Ending

The novel concerns one town and one continuous story.

By the conclusion, Teller's Gorge is left partially broken by the town's wrongdoing and the consequences of Sol's decisions.

There is no complete restoration or definitive moral victory.

Sol and the angel mount their horses and ride away.

They begin discussing Sol's choices in an ancient shared language, provisionally represented as Hebrew.

The discussion continues without interruption through a sudden temporal jump.

The scene changes to 1950s America.

The horses have become a salt delivery van. Their outward identities and circumstances have changed.

But their conversation continues directly from one sentence to the next.

No explanation of the time jump is provided.

The angel chooses their next town.

They travel toward it, but do not arrive within the novel.

This reveals that Teller's Gorge was one case in an ongoing examination of human judgment.

The order and number of Lot's previous trials are deliberately unknown.

He may have undergone one, one hundred, or one thousand.

His purgatory is not necessarily a continuous four-thousand-year experience.

The angel can move them through historical time.

## 13. Current Canon Versus Open Questions

### Established

- The novel's title is *Hard Lot*.
- The Western setting is Teller's Gorge, Colorado.
- The central industry is silver mining, with a salt-consuming refining operation.
- A dominant mining company controls significant portions of the local economy.
- Company scrip or credit and economic dependence are central to the injustice.
- Sol is the biblical Lot.
- His unnamed partner is the angel who once led him out of Sodom.
- Sol is the salesman; the angel selects the destination.
- Lot is being morally examined and understands that fact.
- The town contains genuine wickedness and people who actively resist it.
- Those resisting the company may suffer severely if it collapses.
- The Western story concerns one town.
- The ending leaves the town partially broken.
- An uninterrupted conversation crosses a time jump into a 1950s salt delivery van.
- Each of Lot's assigned identities has an association with salt.
- The count and chronology of previous examinations remain unknown.

### Still to be determined

- The exact year and geographical placement of Teller's Gorge.
- The mine's ore composition and reduction technology.
- The specific scrip, wage, debt, and housing arrangements.
- The precise forms of child labor and possible debt bondage.
- The mine owner's identity and history.
- The reformers and other principal townspeople.
- The particular events that cause Sol to intervene.
- Whether Sol kills anyone, and whom he spares.
- The immediate fate of the mine and company infrastructure.
- The form of the final moral conversation.
- Whether the angel possesses a predetermined standard for success or is also discovering something about justice.
- Whether Lot's purgatory has a definitive endpoint.

# Independent AI Evaluation Request

Please evaluate the current Teller's Gorge setting as a basis for a full-length revisionist Western.

Focus especially on these questions:

1. **Historical credibility:** Is a company-dominated Colorado silver-mining town using scrip and salt-dependent silver processing plausible in the late 1870s–1880s? What specific refinements are necessary?

2. **Economic coherence:** Could a mining company credibly control most employment, housing, water, retail, and local credit without entirely eliminating independent commerce?

3. **Child labor and debt:** What historically defensible arrangements could make children economically captive without relying on anachronistic legal assumptions?

4. **Plot construction:** Can Sol's ordinary sales activities expose the system's abuses naturally, without turning him into a conventional detective?

5. **Moral complexity:** Does the dependence of righteous reformers on the wicked company's continued operation create a genuinely difficult ethical conflict?

6. **Narrative stakes:** What concrete event could force Sol to act rather than simply observe or leave?

7. **Theological connection:** How effectively does the exploitation of families and children connect to Lot's biblical history and Abraham's argument over Sodom?

8. **The ending:** What outcomes could credibly leave Teller's Gorge partially broken without trivializing either the residents' suffering or Sol's moral responsibility?

9. **The central antagonist:** How can the mining company's owner remain convincingly wicked while possessing legitimate accomplishments and rational defenses?

10. **Critical assessment:** Identify the three most serious historical, economic, or narrative weaknesses in the current town design, and propose remedies that preserve the canonical premise.

Do not replace the silver mine with a coal mine, restore the discarded water-only monopoly, introduce Satan as the secret antagonist, or expand the first novel into an episodic sequence of towns.

The goal is a historically grounded mining community whose economic relationships create a genuinely difficult moral examination for Lot.

**The town's wickedness must be real. The suffering of its inhabitants must be real. And the consequences of Lot's judgment must remain real even after he and the angel depart.**

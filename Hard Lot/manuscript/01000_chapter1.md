# Chapter One

The snow had been falling since morning, and the wagon wheels had long since stopped making tracks anyone could follow.

Sol Salazar lifted his hat from his face and squinted at the gray sky. A gust of wind swept across the road, carrying a fresh spray of snow into the wagon bed. He pulled his coat tighter and sat upright, disturbing a little drift that had accumulated against his boots.

“Got a question for you.”

The driver did not turn around. “You've had questions since breakfast.”

“Just one.”

“That's what you said at breakfast.”

Sol brushed the snow from his lap.

“Company.”

“What about it?”

“What does it mean?”

The driver looked over his shoulder.

“Company?”

“In English. What does it mean?”

“Well, that depends.”

“On what?”

“On what you mean by company.”

Sol sighed. “That's what I'm asking you.”

The driver chuckled and adjusted the reins.

“Well, there's company like when folks come visiting. You have company for supper. Or you might say a man's good company, meaning you don't mind having him around.”

“And the other?”

“Other what?”

“Other company.”

“Business company. Men working together. Like us.”

Sol considered that.

“So we're a company.”

“Suppose you could call us that.”

“But you said I'm good company.”

“Did I?”

“Last night.”

“Well, you were less talkative last night.”

Sol pulled his hat back over his eyes.

The driver laughed.

For a while there was nothing but the creaking of the wagon, the muffled clopping of horses, and the soft hiss of snow against canvas.

Then Sol lifted his hat again.

“So a company can own things?”

“Sure can.”

“Land?”

“Certainly.”

“Buildings?”

“Of course.”

“People?”

The driver glanced back.

“Not legally, Sol.”

“Not legally,” Sol repeated.

“No.”

“Then why would a man say he belongs to a company?”

The driver was quiet for a moment.

“That's generally just a manner of speaking. Means he works for them.”

“Ah.”

“You understand?”

“I understand the words.”

The driver smiled faintly and turned back toward the road.

“That's a start.”

Sol watched the snow settle on the driver's shoulders.

“And what happens when he stops working for them?”

“Reckon that depends on the company.”

Sol frowned.

“You English have a very complicated language.”

“Don't blame the language. Men were making things complicated long before English came along.”

“That much I believe.”

The wagon lurched as a wheel dropped into a rut hidden beneath the snow. Sol grabbed the sideboard to steady himself.

“Careful!”

“Road's there whether I can see it or not.”

“That's encouraging.”

“Would you rather drive?”

“No.”

“Then we're agreed.”

Sol settled back and watched the dim outline of the mountains through the falling snow. Somewhere beyond those slopes lay Teller's Gorge, a town that apparently needed salt badly enough to make this miserable journey worthwhile.

He had seen mountains before. Plenty of them. But these were especially inhospitable, their dark shoulders disappearing into clouds that seemed determined to bury the entire country.

“How much farther?”

“Couple miles, maybe.”

“You said that an hour ago.”

“Then we're an hour closer.”

Sol muttered something under his breath.

“What was that?”

“Nothing.”

“Sounded like something.”

“I was praying.”

“Well, don't let me interrupt.”

“I was praying for a better driver.”

The driver's laughter carried over the rattle of the wagon.

A few minutes later he straightened in his seat.

“There.”

Sol followed his gaze.

At first he saw only snow and the dark walls of the gorge. Then, between the trees, a faint yellow light appeared. Another flickered farther up the slope.

“Lanterns,” the driver said. “Looks like we've found it.”

“About time.”

“Thought you wanted to enjoy the scenery.”

“I've enjoyed enough of it.”

The driver clicked his tongue at the horses.

“Welcome to Teller's Gorge, Mr. Salazar.”

The road narrowed as it approached the settlement.

On one side, the mountain rose steeply through stands of dark pine. On the other, the land fell away into a gorge whose depths were hidden by snow and gathering darkness.

Somewhere far below, water rushed over stone.

Sol could hear it but couldn't see it.

A wooden gate blocked the road ahead. Beside it stood a squat guardhouse with a crooked stovepipe belching smoke into the wind.

A man emerged carrying a lantern and a rifle.

“Hold there!”

The driver pulled back on the reins.

The guard approached reluctantly, his coat buttoned up to his chin and a wool scarf wrapped so tightly around his face that only his eyes and the tip of his nose were visible.

“State your business.”

“Salt,” the driver answered.

The guard held up his lantern.

“Salt?”

“Wagonload of it.”

“Who's it for?”

“Whoever's buying.”

The guard looked at the covered cargo.

“Company shipment?”

“Private trade.”

The man stopped moving.

“Private?”

“That's right.”

The guard turned his lantern toward Sol.

“And you?”

“Sol Salazar.”

“Mexican?”

Sol tilted his head.

“Does it matter?”

“Asked you a question.”

“And I asked you one.”

The guard stared at him.

The driver leaned forward.

“Easy, gentlemen. We're just merchants looking for a warm bed and a place to sell our goods. That's all.”

The guard rubbed his gloved hands together.

“Company owns the mill. You'll need to see the superintendent if you want to sell salt there.”

“Happy to,” the driver said.

“Company owns the store, too.”

“Then I expect we'll see him twice.”

The guard didn't smile.

“You got any whiskey?”

“Afraid not.”

“Coffee?”

“Some.”

The guard waited.

The driver waited.

Sol sighed and reached beneath the wagon seat. He produced a small canvas pouch and held it out.

The guard took it, loosened the drawstring, and peered inside.

“Coffee?”

“Unless we've been carrying gravel all this way.”

The guard tucked the pouch into his coat.

“Road's bad beyond the bend. Keep left at the big pine. Right side's washed out.”

“Much obliged.”

“Stable's behind the inn. Don't leave your team out overnight.”

“Wouldn't dream of it.”

The guard lifted the gate's wooden crossbar, dragged it aside, and waved them through.

As the wagon passed, Sol looked toward the guardhouse.

A small boy stood just inside the open doorway, barefoot except for a pair of oversized wool stockings. He couldn't have been more than ten. He carried an armload of split wood nearly as large as himself.

The guard shouted at him.

“Get that door shut, boy! You trying to heat the whole mountain?”

The boy disappeared inside.

Sol turned to his partner.

“His son?”

“Could be.”

“He looked cold.”

“Everybody looks cold, Sol.”

“He didn't have boots.”

“Maybe he left them by the stove.”

Sol looked back, but the guardhouse had already vanished into the snowy darkness.

“Maybe.”

Teller's Gorge appeared gradually through the storm.

A scattering of wooden buildings clung to the mountainside, their steep roofs burdened beneath fresh snow. Lanterns burned behind frosted windows. Smoke rose from chimneys and disappeared almost immediately into the gray evening.

Above the settlement, a sprawling timber structure stretched across the slope, its windows glowing orange. A tall smokestack stood beside it, sending a black plume into the falling snow.

The reduction mill.

Even at this distance, Sol could hear machinery working somewhere inside.

Farther uphill, the mine buildings were only vague shapes against the mountain.

“Busy place,” Sol observed.

“Silver doesn't dig itself.”

“No. But it seems willing to keep men digging after sunset.”

“Money's a powerful encouragement.”

“So I've heard.”

The wagon rolled past a row of small houses. Most were scarcely more than cabins, built close together against the slope. Their windows were shuttered, and only a few showed light.

Beyond them stood a larger house with a broad porch and a wrought-iron fence.

Its windows glowed warmly.

Sol studied the contrast.

“Nice house.”

“Certainly is.”

“Company man?”

“Wouldn't surprise me.”

“How can you tell?”

“Biggest house on the street usually belongs to somebody who can afford it.”

“That doesn't answer my question.”

“Didn't say it would.”

They passed a building with a wide wooden sign hanging over its entrance.

TELLER'S GORGE MERCANTILE & SUPPLY

Beneath the name, in smaller letters, someone had painted:

AUTHORIZED COMPANY ACCOUNTS ACCEPTED

Sol leaned forward.

“There's that word again.”

“Which one?”

“Company.”

“You planning to ask every time you see it?”

“Depends how many meanings it has.”

“Reckon you'll find out.”

A bell rang somewhere farther up the slope.

Several men emerged from a side street, bent against the wind. Their coats and hats were streaked with gray dust, and two carried tin lunch pails.

One of the men stumbled.

Another caught him by the elbow.

They continued downhill together.

Sol watched them pass.

“Mill workers?”

“Likely.”

“Long day.”

“Long days make short nights.”

“And short lives?”

The driver gave him a sideways glance.

“Sometimes.”

Sol said nothing.

They reached a wider street, where a two-story building stood between a blacksmith's shop and a narrow alley. Yellow light spilled from its windows. A weathered sign creaked above the porch.

THE SILVER REST INN

A smaller sign beneath it promised:

ROOMS — MEALS — STABLING

The driver pulled up before the entrance.

“Here we are.”

“Thank heaven.”

“Don't get ahead of yourself. We haven't seen the rooms yet.”

Sol pushed himself upright, his joints stiff from hours in the wagon. He rubbed his hands together and breathed into them.

“Think they'll have a fire?”

“If they don't, I'll build one.”

“Hot food?”

“Almost certainly.”

“Good food?”

“Now you're asking for miracles.”

Sol smiled.

The driver climbed down, his boots sinking into the snow. He secured the reins and walked around the front of the team, patting one horse on the neck before continuing to Sol's side.

Sol had already turned toward the edge of the wagon bed.

His partner stopped beneath him and extended a hand.

Sol looked at it.

The hand was broad and rough, with salt worked into the creases of the palm. The skin was reddened by cold, and an old scar ran across one knuckle.

Sol looked up.

His partner wore a small smile beneath the brim of his snow-covered hat.

For a long moment, neither man moved.

Then Sol smirked.

“You always enjoy helping me, don't you?”

The driver's smile widened.

“Somebody has to.”

Sol took his hand.

The driver steadied him as he stepped down into the snow.

“Come on, Sol. Let's get inside before we freeze.”

Sol brushed snow from his coat and glanced up at the warmly lit windows.

“One more town,” he murmured.

“What was that?”

“Nothing. Just thinking.”

“Well, don't wear yourself out. We've got salt to sell tomorrow.”

Sol looked once more toward the dark silhouette of the mill above the town.

“Of course we do.”

His partner opened the inn door.

Warm air, tobacco smoke, and the smell of cooking meat rolled out into the freezing evening.

Sol stepped inside.

Behind him, the door swung shut against the snow.

I kept this chapter deliberately restrained. The primary job of the opening is to introduce the men as familiar, apparently equal business partners, establish the winter conditions and company-dominated economy, and give Sol his first encounters with people whose circumstances he doesn't yet understand.

The conversation about company introduces the difference between knowing a word and understanding its implications. The guardhouse boy provides an early glimpse of the town's treatment of children without yet proving anything about the boy's circumstances.

Most importantly, the offered hand remains an ordinary gesture on first reading. Its connection to Genesis 19:16 is left entirely for the reader to discover.

The chapter also leaves the business negotiation with the mine, the mechanics of company scrip, and the extent of the town's wickedness for subsequent chapters.

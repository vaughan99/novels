## The Court — Future Novel Notes

### Core Concept

The Court is a **supernatural settlement hidden inside the ruins of an old Roman fortress somewhere near the Northumbrian/Cumbraland border**.

From the ordinary world, the fortress appears ruined:

* collapsing Roman walls,
* fallen timber,
* rubble,
* weeds,
* wreckage,
* nothing apparently inhabited.

Inside, a permanent glamour preserves or overlays a functioning supernatural court.

The glamour does **not merely hide the place visually**. It removes it from ordinary geographical logic. The Court no longer fully exists in the “plain” world.

You cannot find it by coordinates.

You must find it **narratively**.

---

## Reaching the Court

Directions to the Court sound impossible because supernatural creatures are not describing physical distance. They are describing a **story-path**.

Example:

> “Go farther than the sunset, but no farther than the next hill.”

Taken geographically, this is nonsense.

Taken narratively, it may be perfectly precise.

Other possible directions:

* Take the road you did not choose.
* Cross the stream before you reach it.
* Walk until the raven lies.
* Do not look for the gate until you have already passed through.
* Go where the dead Roman still keeps watch.

To enter, a traveler must effectively **conceive of a story that fools or satisfies the glamour**.

The story provides the route.

A traveler might tell themselves they are:

* looking for a lost sheep,
* returning something stolen,
* following a dead man home,
* seeking shelter,
* carrying something to a lord who no longer exists.

If the narrative fits strongly enough, geography yields to it.

---

# Origin-Bringers

Occasionally, ordinary humans accidentally discover a new story that allows them to enter the Court unaided.

These humans are called:

## **Origin-Bringers**

They are honored guests.

Their importance comes from the fact that supernatural beings often inhabit established stories, rules, roles, and traditions, while humans have an unusual capacity to **invent new narrative possibilities**.

An Origin-Bringer has done something extraordinary:

> They have created a route that did not previously exist.

Possible metaphysical significance:

**Wyrd says:** this is where the road leads.
**Origin-Bringer says:** here is a road that did not exist before.

Origin-Bringers may automatically receive:

* guest-right,
* market protection,
* freedom from certain forms of predation,
* right of audience,
* elevated social status.

### Redcap Rule

Redcaps may hunt ordinary humans.

They are **forbidden to hunt Origin-Bringers**.

Possible exchange:

> “He’s mine.”
>
> “Not anymore.”
>
> “I found him first.”
>
> “He found us.”

The prohibition is not necessarily humanitarian. Origin-Bringers are too rare and valuable to waste.

---

# The Physical Court

The interior does not need to look like an accurately restored Roman fortress.

Instead, it can be a **palimpsest of what generations of supernatural inhabitants think a court should be**.

Thus:

* Roman stone,
* Saxon benches,
* Norse beams,
* Brittonic carvings,
* impossible rooms,
* architectural elements inherited from stories rather than history.

Possible reuse of Roman spaces:

* **principia** → audience hall,
* barracks → lodgings / workshops,
* granaries → supernatural warehouses,
* strongroom → debt archive,
* bathhouse → prestigious or uncanny social space,
* armory → smith-market,
* gates → jurisdictional thresholds.

The glamour may preserve functional spaces while the plain-world structure remains collapsed.

---

# General Economic Principle

Avoid a conventional fantasy/D&D magic market.

The Court should **not** primarily sell:

* potions,
* wands,
* enchanted swords,
* generic spell scrolls,
* magical trinkets.

Physical supernatural goods can exist — unicorn horn, monster parts, strange bones, etc. — but they should be peripheral.

The Court's real economy trades in:

## **claims upon reality**

Its commodities are:

* rights,
* permissions,
* obligations,
* stories,
* access,
* memories,
* reputations,
* dreams,
* interpretations,
* futures,
* favors,
* narrative conditions.

Not everything can be purchased with everything else. Different markets use different forms of value.

---

# Major Classes of Supernatural Goods

## Dream-Rights

The legal right to enter someone's dreams.

May specify:

* particular person,
* family,
* house,
* season,
* number of nights,
* type of dream,
* right to observe,
* right to speak,
* right to influence imagery.

Example:

> “Three nights of speaking-right, no frightening.”

---

## Nightmare-Rights

Separate from ordinary dream-rights.

They grant permission to frighten, oppress, pursue, paralyze, suffocate, or otherwise produce nightmares.

This allows legal disputes:

> “You bought dreams.”
>
> “He was frightened.”
>
> “Exactly. I own nightmares.”

Nightmare-rights may be sold, inherited, wagered, or seized for debt.

An ancient family curse may simply be a nightmare-right that has changed hands repeatedly.

---

## Glamours

Not bottled illusion spells.

A glamour-worker sells **commissioned alterations of perception or appearance**.

Examples:

* Make my house appear abandoned for seven winters.
* Make travelers remember a different face.
* Make this road appear to bend elsewhere.
* Make everyone believe I arrived yesterday.
* Make a doorway appear where none is noticed.

Payment depends upon duration, audience, complexity, and how much reality must be persuaded.

---

## Recognition / Forgetting

Rights or services involving whether someone is remembered or recognized.

Examples:

* the right to be remembered by a particular person,
* anonymity from strangers,
* removal of a face from memory,
* preservation of one memory after all others forget.

Potential specialist:

> “I can make you forget his face. Not what he did.”

---

## Introductions

A supernatural introduction can itself be property.

Example:

> “Three introductions to the Lady Beneath the Mere.”

Each introduction is consumed when used.

The broker guarantees access, not favor.

---

## Audience-Rights

The right to be heard by a powerful supernatural being for a fixed amount of time.

Extremely valuable.

Again:

**audience does not guarantee agreement.**

---

## Proxy Agents to Gods and Mythological Beings

Specialists who act as intermediaries.

Their commodity is:

* access,
* etiquette,
* correct forms of petition,
* ritual competence,
* interpretation,
* relationship capital.

Possible specialties:

* river-beings,
* dead kings,
* saints,
* Norse gods,
* storms,
* land-wights,
* ancestral powers.

A proxy does not control the being.

They know **how to get the request heard**.

Possible conflict:

> “I represent the hill and the shepherds.”
>
> “Both?”
>
> “Usually.”
>
> “And when they disagree?”
>
> “That costs more.”

---

# Narrative Commodities

## Names

Not necessarily “true names = magical passwords.”

Names may instead provide:

* social existence,
* recognizability,
* legal standing,
* prestige,
* belonging.

A nameless creature could lease or purchase a usable name.

---

## Titles

Titles may confer real supernatural privileges.

Examples:

* Keeper of the Third Ford
* Guest of the Hill
* Friend of Crows

Some are prestigious.

Some are functional.

Some are nearly worthless.

---

## Reputation

Fear, honor, notoriety, or trust can be bought or transferred.

Example:

> “I sell you the fear of my name in three villages.”

Afterward, people may instinctively fear the buyer.

---

## Stories About Oneself

A creature may pay others to propagate a particular version of an event.

Example:

> “Tell them I spared you.”

The story may alter:

* reputation,
* future behavior toward the creature,
* the creature's own supernatural identity.

---

## Beginnings

A beginning can itself be created or purchased.

Someone may arrange for an event to become:

> “the beginning of the story in which…”

This is potentially very powerful.

---

## Endings

A specialist may provide an ending to something that otherwise cannot conclude.

Alternatively, an ending could be removed.

Potential applications:

* curses,
* marriages,
* feuds,
* hauntings,
* obligations.

---

## Version-Rights

After an event occurs, competing parties may purchase or contest the right to determine **which version becomes the remembered story**.

This could be among the Court's most valuable commodities.

The corpse, boots, and silver of a dead traveler are valuable.

But perhaps the most valuable asset is:

> **Who gets to say what happened to him?**

---

# Location and Movement Commodities

## Road-Rights

Permission to use supernatural routes.

A road may exist only for:

* widows,
* liars,
* children,
* people carrying bread,
* people returning something,
* those who have never traveled it before.

Road-right can be sold.

---

## Finding-Rights

Not the lost object itself.

The right to be the person who **finds it**.

Possible targets:

* rings,
* graves,
* people,
* horses,
* roads,
* forgotten buildings.

---

## Being Lost / Being Unfindable

Someone can purchase:

> “One night of being unfindable.”

Pursuers repeatedly choose the wrong road.

---

## Threshold-Rights

Permission to cross:

* a house threshold,
* churchyard boundary,
* grave boundary,
* ford,
* hall doorway,
* magical border.

Invitation may itself be transferable property.

---

## Hospitality / Guest-Right

Supernaturally enforceable hospitality.

Being someone's guest may confer real protection.

This can be bought, inherited, or negotiated.

---

## Sanctuary

Temporary immunity from certain pursuers or classes of beings.

The value depends upon who guarantees it.

---

# Personal / Emotional Commodities

## Sleep

A sleepless creature may purchase one genuine human night's sleep.

The human remains awake in exchange.

---

## Rest

Different from sleep.

A ghost might buy:

> “One hour in which I no longer have to keep watch.”

---

## Fear

Specific fears may be transferred.

Not generic “fear points.”

Examples:

* fear of wolves,
* fear of bells,
* fear of water,
* fear of a particular name.

---

## Courage

Should be narrowly bounded.

Example:

> “Enough courage to open one door.”

---

## Sorrow

A person may sell the pain attached to a memory without losing the memory itself.

The buyer may require sorrow for:

* ritual,
* prophecy,
* disguise,
* food,
* art.

---

## Homesickness

Someone can sell homesickness.

Danger:

They may later discover they can no longer feel that anywhere is home.

---

## Belonging

A being can purchase the right to be treated by a place as if it belongs there.

Consequences:

* dogs stop barking,
* paths stop resisting,
* doors seem familiar,
* local spirits accept its presence.

---

## Exile

The opposite.

Someone can be narratively exiled from a place so that roads themselves refuse to deliver them there.

---

# Information / Truth Commodities

## Questions

The right to ask one question and receive a truthful answer.

Particularly valuable from creatures otherwise incapable of straightforward honesty.

---

## Answers Without Questions

Someone may possess an answer whose question has been lost.

These can circulate as bizarre speculative assets.

---

## Secrets

Ownership of a secret may be transferable.

If sold, perhaps the previous owner becomes unable to reveal it because:

> it is no longer theirs to tell.

---

## Silence

Silence can be purchased around:

* events,
* places,
* names,
* individuals.

Example:

> “No bird will speak of what happens here until morning.”

---

## Lies

Professionally constructed lies that supernatural systems accept as true until a specified condition breaks them.

This connects naturally to the Court's entrance glamour.

---

## Alibis

A glamour/story specialist can create an alibi convincing enough that witnesses sincerely remember the buyer being somewhere else.

---

## Witness

Recognized supernatural testimony may have binding power.

A professional witness could function almost like a notary.

---

# Obligations and Debt

This should probably be one of the Court's deepest markets.

## Promises

Promises can be sold.

Example:

> “He owes me one night's shelter.”

That promise can become someone else's asset.

---

## Favors

Obligations circulate like currency.

Elswyth might discover that the favor she owes one creature has been sold to another.

---

## Debts of Kindness

Potentially more powerful than ordinary debt.

Someone once spared someone's child.

That obligation may retain supernatural weight generations later.

---

## Oaths

Unused or future oaths can have value.

Some beings may have reputations for particularly binding promises.

---

## Grudges

A grievance can be transferred.

A monster hunting someone may never have met them.

It simply bought the grandfather's grudge.

---

## Forgiveness

Not theological absolution.

The relinquishment of a supernatural grievance.

Someone can buy forgiveness from a river, hill, household spirit, etc.

---

## Blame

Responsibility for an act could potentially be transferred.

Dangerous commodity.

The metaphysical consequences follow whoever legally/narratively owns the blame.

---

# Small-Scale Fate Commodities

## Luck

Keep luck extremely specific.

Examples:

* Your next thrown stone lands where intended.
* The next horse you buy will be sound.
* You will not draw the shortest lot.
* The next door you choose will be the right one.

Avoid generic +luck effects.

---

## Misfortune

Likewise transferable only in specific pieces.

Examples:

* my next dropped cup,
* one sprained ankle,
* one winter fever,
* one broken wheel.

---

## Coincidences

A seller can arrange a bounded coincidence:

> “You will happen to sit beside him at supper.”

No guarantee what happens afterward.

---

## Meetings

The right or guarantee that two paths cross once.

---

# Firsts and Lasts

These may be valuable for reasons humans don't understand.

Examples of **firsts**:

* first apple of an orchard,
* first cry of a newborn,
* first snow on a roof,
* first lie told in a new house.

Examples of **lasts**:

* last breath of a dying fire,
* last leaf of autumn,
* last word spoken in an abandoned hall,
* last song of a dying person.

---

# Shadows, Reflections, Echoes, Scents

## Shadows

Possible uses:

* disguise,
* concealment,
* false presence,
* leaving one's shadow somewhere else.

---

## Reflections

Can be borrowed or sold.

A glamour-worker might use another person's reflection to make a convincing duplicate.

Seller temporarily has none.

---

## Echoes

Ownership of the next echo from a particular location.

Useful for:

* false commands,
* hauntings,
* messages,
* traps.

---

## Scents

Especially relevant to supernatural recognition.

A person's supernatural “smell” could be:

* concealed,
* duplicated,
* transferred,
* forged.

This may connect to creatures recognizing someone as “wrong.”

---

# Death-Related Markets

## Death Notices

The right to know when a specific person dies.

Very valuable in a premodern communication world.

---

## Mourning-Rights

Who has the right to mourn someone, or whose mourning the dead will hear.

---

## Ghost-Rights

The future right to haunt a particular place.

A living person could sell their eventual haunting rights while still alive.

---

## Corpse-Rights

The corpse itself can have divided supernatural claims:

* eyes,
* name,
* shadow,
* grave,
* visitation,
* dreams of descendants.

Keep body-part commerce secondary to these more interesting rights.

---

# Prediction Markets

The Court has genuine markets in future events.

They are not merely gambling.

They are attempting to **price inevitability**.

Possible contracts concern:

* marriages,
* births,
* wars,
* kingship,
* journeys,
* storms,
* deaths,
* survival.

Historically within the fictional world, real diviners once helped anchor these markets.

---

# Death Futures

One particularly grim class of prediction contract.

Examples:

* dies before winter,
* dies by violence,
* dies away from home,
* survives the coming battle,
* dies before another named person,
* bloodline ends within three generations.

These are **not assassination contracts**.

They are claims upon expected future events.

Possible related products:

* survival contracts,
* joint-life contracts,
* battle mortality pools,
* death options,
* bloodline futures.

---

# Wyrd Crisis

The Court exists during or after the beginning of the **death of wyrd**.

This causes a major economic and metaphysical crisis.

## Diviners

Real diviners are becoming less reliable.

More disturbingly:

### genuine diviners may be dying.

Nobody initially knows why.

The strongest diviners may die first because they were most deeply connected to wyrd.

Charlatans survive because they were never connected to anything.

Thus:

> the dying of the diviners proves the diviners were real.

Eventually there may be **more fortune-tellers than ever**, while almost no genuine prophecy remains.

---

## Divination Market Effects

A formerly bustling divination market empties.

Possible visual:

A diviner once selling:

> **FUTURES**

crosses it out and writes:

> **PASTS**

because the past still stays where it is.

Another begins selling probabilities instead of certainties.

---

## Old Prophecies

Prophecies made while wyrd was healthy become valuable antiques.

They may be auctioned and traded.

Not because everyone trusts them completely, but because they are remnants of a period when destiny actually held.

---

## Prediction-Market Collapse

Long-term future contracts become unstable.

Short-term causality still works reasonably well.

Formerly “safe” wyrd-backed predictions become toxic assets.

The Court may experience something like a supernatural financial panic.

---

# Political Crisis: The Missing King

The Court's King is trapped inside **Monanleoht**.

The Court has functioned without him long enough for a substitute political order to develop.

When Elswyth arrives carrying the blade, the King's prison has effectively returned to his own realm.

But:

> **finding the jail cell does not mean anyone knows how to open it.**

Nor does everyone necessarily want it opened.

Possible factions:

### Restorationists

Want the King released and the old order restored.

### Regency / Successor Faction

Has accumulated authority during his absence and may publicly favor rescue while quietly delaying it.

### Market Interests

Fear that restoration will invalidate contracts, licenses, privileges, debts, monopolies, dream-rights, hunting rights, etc.

### Redcaps

Potentially divided depending on how much freedom they gained during the King's absence.

### Diviners / Wyrd Faction

Some suspect restoring the King could repair the collapsing order.

Others fear his imprisonment is connected to wyrd's death.

### Origin-Bringer Faction

Interested in humans like Elswyth because conventional supernatural rules may be incapable of solving the prison.

---

# The King's Release Problem

The blade is not merely an object containing him.

It is a **narrative prison**.

Possible release conditions might involve:

* the original jailer,
* forgiveness,
* return to the site of imprisonment,
* retelling the true story,
* completing an unfinished bargain,
* replacing the prison,
* inventing a new ending.

The most thematically powerful possibility:

> Everyone in the Court understands the old story too well to escape it.

An **Origin-Bringer** may be necessary because only someone capable of creating a new story can introduce a release condition that did not previously exist.

Thus Elswyth's arrival is politically explosive.

---

# Redcaps and the Court

Redcaps are associated with the fortress/castle.

Elswyth theorizes that they:

* hunt outside,
* return to the fortress,
* maintain a “court” there.

She is mostly correct.

The redcaps are not necessarily the entirety of the Court population.

They may function economically as:

* hunters,
* enforcers,
* suppliers,
* scouts,
* collectors of outside goods,
* brokers of violence.

Possible imports:

* meat,
* blood,
* human possessions,
* iron,
* information,
* captives.

They are dangerous but subject to Court law.

Notably:

> **They may not hunt Origin-Bringers.**

---

# Ordinary Humans Within the Court

A handful of humans may live there permanently.

Some entered accidentally by daydreaming their way into a valid narrative path.

Potential roles:

* interpreters of human behavior,
* merchants in mundane goods,
* story-testers,
* negotiators with churches,
* witnesses,
* translators,
* professional liars,
* outside-world buyers,
* advisers on whether a glamour will actually fool humans.

Human imagination may become increasingly valuable as wyrd dies.

The old supernatural world depends heavily upon:

* inherited stories,
* fixed roles,
* established patterns,
* prophecy.

Humans are unusually capable of asking:

> “What if it goes another way?”

That may make them economically and politically more important as the crisis develops.

---

# Governing Aesthetic

The Court should feel less like:

> **a fantasy marketplace where monsters sell magical items**

and more like:

> **a medieval market, court, exchange, law-house, betting floor, brokerage, and supernatural bureaucracy dealing in things humans normally don't realize can be owned.**

Its strangeness should come from how seriously everyone takes these transactions.

A stall selling unicorn horns may exist.

But across the aisle someone is selling:

> the right to haunt a bishop's dreams for three Thursdays,

and that is where the serious money is.

### Core principle

> **The Court trades in claims upon reality.**

And beneath everything is the emerging crisis:

> **The old world was built around stories whose endings could once be known. Now the endings are coming loose.**

# 10th-Century Northumbria / Cumbraland Supernatural Creatures

The important principle is that there is **no clean modern “fairy taxonomy.”** Anglo-Saxon, Norse, Brittonic, and Christian ideas overlap. The same being might be called an elf, demon, giant, land-spirit, or simply “a thing” depending on who encounters it.

Use **old ingredients, not later folkloric categories**.

## Particularly strong choices for Northumbria/Cumbraland

### Þyrs

Probably one of the strongest general-purpose monsters.

It can be:

* giant,
* ugly humanoid,
* solitary,
* demonic,
* magical,
* fen-dwelling,
* ruin-haunting.

Most importantly, nobody needs to agree exactly what it is.

A Christian may call it a demon.
Ketill might call it a *þurs*.
Osric might call it a *þyrs*.

They can all be describing the same creature.

---

### Nicor

Ideal for the Lake District and western Northumbria.

Do not make it a standardized lake monster. It is better if the knowledge is:

> Something lives in that water.
> People disappear there.
> Do not swim after dusk.

Possible forms can remain inconsistent:

* long-necked,
* serpentine,
* vaguely humanoid,
* seal-like,
* enormous fish,
* something never fully seen.

---

### Ælf

Very useful because modern readers arrive with the wrong expectation.

A 10th-century elf can be:

* beautiful,
* dangerous,
* invisible,
* associated with sudden pain,
* illness,
* seduction,
* supernatural attack.

This gives you room for something genuinely frightening while still calling it an elf.

---

### Eoten / Jötunn

Excellent for the Anglo-Scandinavian north.

The linguistic overlap itself is useful. English and Norse speakers can recognize that they are probably talking about related ideas while using different words.

A creature could even object to the classification.

---

### Wyrm

One of the safest monsters to use.

A wyrm does not need wings.

It could be:

* gigantic snake,
* grave-serpent,
* cave monster,
* dragon,
* poisonous thing under a hill,
* hoard guardian,
* corpse-eater.

This is probably more period-flavored than automatically reaching for the modern four-legged winged dragon.

---

### Roman-Ruin Giant

Especially good for your setting.

People live among enormous Roman remains whose builders are long dead and whose technical scale can appear almost superhuman.

That gives easy folk explanations:

> “Giants built it.”

And somebody more historically knowledgeable can say:

> “Romans built it.”

without necessarily settling the supernatural question.

---

### Restless Dead

Useful, but keep them culturally messy rather than inventing a clean zombie species.

Possibilities:

* corpse walking home,
* dead warrior returning,
* grave occupant attacking intruders,
* drowned person returning wet,
* dead person appearing physically rather than as a transparent ghost.

Different cultures can interpret the same event differently.

---

## Brittonic / Cumbrian Layer

Cumbraland gives you additional freedom because of the overlap among:

* Brittonic culture,
* Northumbrian English culture,
* Scandinavian settlement,
* Christianity.

The **Otherworld** concept is particularly useful.

Early Welsh/Brittonic material supports ideas involving:

* otherworld realms,
* supernatural journeys,
* uncanny women,
* prophetic beings,
* strange hunts,
* altered geography,
* supernatural feasts,
* places accessible through unusual conditions.

For your purposes, it is safer to use the **underlying concept** than later standardized folkloric names.

---

## Creatures/labels to treat cautiously

These may be usable in fiction, but should not be presented as securely documented 943 folklore in their later familiar forms:

* **redcap**
* **brownie**
* **boggart**
* **kelpie**
* **Tylwyth Teg**
* **gwragedd y llyn**
* **Cŵn Annwn**
* later standardized fairy courts
* later Black Shuck traditions

The concepts behind some of these may be much older, but the recognizable named traditions are often documented centuries later.

For *The Cynn*, that does not make them unusable. It just means the novel is **projecting an older origin backward**, rather than reconstructing a securely attested 10th-century taxonomy.

# Redcaps in Your Setting

Current working interpretation:

# General Worldbuilding Rule

The best supernatural encounter should usually allow multiple simultaneous explanations.

For example, something encountered beside a ruined Roman fort might be called:

* a **þyrs** by Osric,
* a **þurs** by Ketill,
* a **demon** by Cynewulf,
* an **old thing of the hill** by a Cumbrian,
* and something entirely different by itself.

None of those labels necessarily has to be wrong.

That keeps the supernatural world feeling **pre-taxonomic, regional, and lived-in** rather than like a monster manual.

Fighting a demon whose magic is that it becomes the thing that the culture of the viewer has prepared for it. Ketill figures out that out and goes through a sequence while everyone else tries to avoid getting killed.

Yes — this is much better than having Ketill remember some obscure monster-rule.

The key is that he is not solving the encounter through **specialized demonology**. He is solving it through **a story he already knows well enough to inhabit**.

That makes the mechanic feel earned.

> Ketill concentrated. The creature grabbed Ketill's body.
>
> Then Ketill opened his eyes.
>
> “My name Ketill.”
>
> The creature slowly put him down and walked away.

That is wonderfully eerie because from everyone else’s point of view, absolutely nothing explanatory happens. Ketill gets grabbed, says his own name, and the monster just decides the encounter is over.

Then later:

> “What did you do?”
>
> “Pretend to be Finn from story. Water ask name. I give it. It no bother me.”

And now the reader understands what happened: He **stepped into the role occupied by a person in a story whose encounter with the supernatural had a known resolution**.

That is a major improvement to the whole mechanic.

It means the demon is responding not simply to cultural identity—

> *I am Norse.*
> *I am Magyar.*
> *I am Serk.*

—but to **narrative identity**:

> *I am the Finn in this particular story.*

And therefore the demon gets forced into the complementary role.

That opens the system enormously while making it less arbitrary. Ketill cannot simply think, “I am someone whose monster is easy to kill.” He needs an actual story he remembers, including enough of its structure to reproduce the role.

The crucial distinction becomes:

**The demon does not become what you imagine. It becomes what your story requires it to be.**

And Ketill doesn't control the ending either. He has to know the ending and play his part correctly.

That is much more original than the boggart-like version.

It also makes Ketill specifically suited to solving the problem because he has collected **foreign stories**, not merely foreign facts. His cosmopolitanism becomes operational:

> Somewhere in all those stories he has heard is one in which a person survives this kind of encounter.

And he searches for that story rather than searching for a better weapon.

I particularly like that the successful solution is **“My name Ketill.”** He pretends to be Finn only long enough to establish the story, but when the supernatural thing asks the question the story demands, he answers truthfully as himself.

So he doesn't become Finn.

He borrows Finn's **place in the story**.

That distinction is excellent.

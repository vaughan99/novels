## Book Two Seed: Osric’s Return to Hexham and the Judgment That Follows

Osric’s return to Hexham is not an accidental capture, nor the collapse of an escape. By the time he reaches the lands of his lord, he has already made a private decision that none of his companions fully understands. He intends to answer the charges against him.

The others believe they have brought him home so that he may find the remains of his household, recover what can be recovered, and bury his wife and children. They know that he wants to reach Hexham before Galindo’s fyrd, but they assume this is because he still means to escape the men hunting him. They do not realize that he has been racing toward judgment rather than away from it.

Osric knows what will happen if the hostile members of the fyrd reach Hexham first. Long before any formal hearing begins, they will tell everyone who will listen that he stole his neighbors’ grain, killed his wife and children, destroyed his own house, committed unnatural acts with livestock, and fled because he was guilty. By the time a witan or lordly hearing is convened, the accusations may already have hardened into reputation. Men will enter the hall believing they know what sort of man stands before them.

That is the real race.

Osric is not primarily trying to beat Galindo. In fact, he may trust Galindo’s fairness more than he trusts the fyrdmen’s stories. Galindo has always been severe, disciplined, and conscientious. When he took Osric into custody, he did so because the yard had become dangerous. The angry men around Osric had already turned accusation into punishment and might have beaten him to death had Galindo not intervened decisively.

Galindo’s act of binding Osric was humiliating, but it was also protective. He needed to establish immediate and unquestioned control over Osric before the mob could claim him. He did not take Osric because he had already judged him guilty. He took him because the charges were grave, the danger was immediate, and Osric could not safely be left among the fyrd.

From Osric’s perspective, Galindo reduced him to a thrall and placed a rope around his wrists at the lowest moment of his life. From Galindo’s perspective, he made Osric unmistakably his prisoner so that no other man could strike him without challenging the lord’s authority.

Both memories are true.

Galindo would later be able to say:

> “I made you mine to command long enough to stop them killing you.”

Osric would still be entitled to answer:

> “You put a rope around my wrists.”

Galindo’s fairness should remain intact throughout the second novel. He may be angry about the escape. He may insist that Osric deceived his men, resisted custody, and passed his fyrd under a fraudulent disguise. But he will distinguish what he personally knows from what others merely alleged.

At the hearing, when asked whether Osric murdered his family, Galindo should answer that he does not know. When asked whether Osric stole the grain, Galindo should say he did not witness it. When asked whether Osric destroyed his own house, he should say he saw no such act. Galindo brought Osric into custody because the accusations required judgment, not because accusation itself was judgment.

He may become one of the most important witnesses in Osric’s favor precisely because he refuses to become Osric’s ally. His value lies in his discipline.

He can say:

> “You told me many things. I brought a prisoner to judgment because such charges must be heard. I did not bring your certainty as evidence.”

The bitter irony is that Osric’s companions have also become witnesses without initially understanding that role. They saw the ruined house. They saw the snow barrow. They helped recover and bury Osric’s kin. They saw his conduct upon returning to the remains of his life. They can testify not to every hidden cause, but to what the ground looked like, what had been destroyed, what had survived, and how Osric behaved when confronted with the dead.

They cannot prove every element of his innocence. They cannot necessarily explain the entire chain of hearth failure, improperly boiled elderberry syrup, death, collapse, fire, snow, rumor, and accusation. But they can expose the difference between what was seen and what was invented.

The hostile fyrdmen can say what they believed before they left Hexham.

Galindo can say what he witnessed during Osric’s custody.

Osric’s companions can say what they found when they returned to his home.

That creates the true structure of the case.

When the Hexham hearth guard appears at the end of the first book, the party initially reacts as though another escape must begin. Hands move toward weapons. One-Eye loosens Barrow’s Edge in its sheath. Ketill prepares himself. Murdock begins to think about the wagon and the horses. They expect Osric to turn.

Instead, he stops.

The twist is not that the guard found him. The twist is that Osric has been expecting them.

Cynewulf may be the first to understand.

> “You meant to do this.”

Osric’s answer should remain quiet:

> “I meant to bury them first.”

That is enough for the end of the first book.

Osric had one obligation he would not surrender to the lord, Galindo, the witan, or the men who hated him. He would not stand judgment while his family remained unburied. He would return to them as a free man, do what the dead required, and only then answer the living.

When the captain of the hearth guard orders him to surrender, Osric does not deny that the guard has come for him. He does not draw Barrow’s Edge. He does not place his companions between himself and Hexham.

He separates their cause from his own.

> “The others have no quarrel with Hexham. This dispute is mine.”

The captain may point out that they aided an escaped prisoner. Osric can answer that they aided a man returning to the judgment he had been promised.

He asks to be brought before the Lord of Hexham unbound. Unbound does not mean free. He can surrender his weapons, including Barrow’s Edge if necessary, and walk surrounded by armed hearthguards. His insistence is symbolic and political. He will not enter the hall visually presented as a condemned murderer and thrall before the lord has heard a word.

His request might be:

> “I will go willingly, if I may speak to the Lord of Hexham. Unbound.”

The captain may object that Osric is in no position to bargain.

Osric’s answer need not be elaborate.

> “I am not running.”

Or:

> “A fugitive would have chosen another road.”

The first book should end at or near that moment. The lord, the hearing, the political accusations, the disposition of the house, the cattle, the field, and the banishment all belong to the second novel.

Book one ends with Osric standing his ground.

Book two begins with the consequences of standing there.

---

Osric’s first argument before the Lord of Hexham is that he did not flee judgment.

He escaped Galindo’s custody, certainly. He crossed the country ahead of the fyrd. He deceived Galindo and passed the force on Dere Street. But his destination matters.

> “My lord, I did not flee. I raced to be the first one here.”

That line attacks the central meaning of the accusation. A man fleeing judgment does not race toward the hall where judgment will occur.

The lord may reply:

> “You escaped lawful custody.”

Osric can answer:

> “I escaped a chain, my lord. Not judgment.”

Or:

> “A fugitive runs away from judgment. I ran toward it.”

Yet Osric’s true concern was never simply Galindo’s official report. Galindo would have brought the entire fyrd with him, including the men who had already condemned Osric socially and morally.

Cynewulf might ask:

> “You feared Galindo would lie?”

Osric’s answer:

> “No. I feared he would arrive honestly, surrounded by liars.”

That sentence summarizes the whole danger.

Galindo would bring facts.

The fyrdmen would bring a story.

Osric came first so the story would not become fact before anyone heard him speak.

---

Osric should also request that Cynewulf be allowed to speak beside him or on his behalf.

This should not resemble a modern attorney-client arrangement. Cynewulf is not a barrister. He is a priest, an educated man, a manipulator of documents, a skilled speaker, and someone capable of distinguishing between promises, law, rumor, status, custody, and evidence.

Osric knows what happened to him.

Cynewulf knows how other men will name what happened.

A strong formulation would be:

> “I ask leave for Cynewulf to speak beside me. I know what happened. He knows what men will try to call it.”

Osric does not surrender his own voice. He asks that Cynewulf handle the portions involving procedure, wording, documents, status, promises, and the contradictions between accusation and proof.

Osric also asks that the cases of his companions be heard separately rather than treating the party as a single criminal band.

Murdock has a narrow but defensible dispute of his own. He was contracted as baggage master. Once the baggage train and its wagons were destroyed, he argues that the thing he had been hired to master no longer existed.

> “I was baggage master, my lord. Once the wagons were gone, so was my bargain.”

If challenged that his contract did not burn with the wagons, he can answer:

> “Then tell me what I was still contracted to master.”

His argument is practical rather than moral. He does not deny the contract. He argues that its object ceased to exist.

The others have even less direct quarrel with Hexham. They helped Osric travel, survive, bury his kin, and return. Their testimony may be relevant, but their legal position should not automatically collapse into his.

---

The Lord of Hexham should not be written as a craven or foolish tyrant.

He may be severe, proud, politically calculating, and insulated from the consequences of his decisions, but he is still capable of judgment. He does not need to be secretly saintly. He merely needs to understand that lordship survives through credible decisions, not through condemning every vulnerable man placed before him.

The lord’s earlier choice is one of the deeper conflicts waiting beneath Osric’s personal case.

The Lord of Hexham had sent his fyrd to Leicester as payment of a debt or obligation owed to King Edmund’s court. The exact nature of that debt remains open. It may have involved royal confirmation of his authority, failure to provide earlier service, political surety after shifting Northumbrian loyalties, land confirmed by the crown, or some other obligation by which Hexham owed conspicuous military support.

The important point is that the debt belonged to the lord, while the men of Hexham paid it in marching, hunger, siege, wounds, and death.

Oda, Archbishop of Canterbury, then used the military advantage created at Leicester to secure a more protracted diplomatic success. The fyrd had helped create the leverage. Oda converted that leverage into political terms.

Those terms may have been strategically wise. They may have prevented a worse war. They may even have served Northumbria in the long run.

But Osric and the fyrd suffered the immediate cost while the Lord of Hexham suffered none of the bodily consequences.

Osric’s grievance is not simply that the lord made the wrong decision. The Lord of Hexham did not personally negotiate Oda’s settlement. Osric’s accusation is that his lord sent men into another powerful man’s political bargain, remained at home, and failed to understand what became of those who bore the result.

The cleanest statement is:

> “You owed the king, my lord. We paid him.”

Or:

> “The debt was yours. The march was ours. The siege was ours. And when the peace turned bitter, the chains were mine.”

Osric can also argue that the lord placed Hexham’s men in battle against other Northumbrians with whom they traded in ordinary life. The armies allied with York were not distant strangers. They were men from communities connected through markets, roads, kinship, livestock, iron, wool, Church travel, and seasonal exchange.

The lord may say:

> “They chose Edmund’s enemy.”

Osric can answer:

> “For one season. The rest of the year, they are our neighbors.”

Or:

> “You sent us to fight men we bargain with in every market.”

This does not make Osric a modern pacifist. He understands military obligation and political allegiance. His objection is that elite debts and changing loyalties created a battlefield between men whose daily lives remained connected.

His most dangerous accusation may be:

> “You sent us to kill our neighbors so the king would call you loyal.”

That may be too direct for the first hearing, but it belongs somewhere in the larger confrontation.

The lord should have a credible defense. He can argue that King Edmund required service, that Hexham’s standing depended upon demonstrating loyalty, that a lord cannot ignore a royal summons, and that military victory at a single wall is not the same thing as peace.

Osric can concede the strategic argument without surrendering the moral one.

> “Perhaps the peace was wise, my lord. But wisdom is easily claimed by the man who does not bleed for it.”

Or:

> “I understand why Oda bargained. I do not think you understand what it cost us.”

The debate should remain messy. The lord may have done what political survival required. Osric may still be right that the consequences were distributed downward.

---

Osric’s transformation will be startling to the Lord of Hexham.

The last version of Osric known to Hexham was a broken ceorl reduced to custody, shame, grief, and helplessness. Now he enters the lord’s hall dressed and armed like a man of a court guard, accompanied by a priest, mercenaries, foreigners, a frightened girl with a steel crossbow, a wagon master, and men whose stories will make little immediate sense.

The lord may ask:

> “So. You were a thrall. An empty rag of a man. And now you appear in my hall dressed like a court guard. How is this?”

Osric’s answer should remain understated:

> “I have… interesting friends.”

The pause carries the impossibility of explaining everything at once: forge-elves, the Cynn, the bathhouse, Barrow’s Edge, false saints, blind pilgrims, Galindo’s Andalusian, and every strange mile between Leicester and Hexham.

---

Osric may also be less afraid of death than anyone in the hall expects.

He drew Barrow’s Edge from the barrow and survived. In his own mind, he may feel that he has already cheated death once. This does not have to make him believe he is immortal. It may make him feel that his life has already crossed some boundary and now carries an obligation.

He may believe the blade chose him.

He may believe he was spared for a reason.

He may believe that carrying Barrow’s Edge means he must do right by it.

That belief brings him dangerously close to proving Odin correct in the larger debate. But that is not a flaw. Odin is allowed to win a point.

Freyr does not need to deny that destiny called Osric. He can concede it while refusing Odin’s larger conclusion.

> “I concede that destiny may call a man. But it cannot change his season.”

That is Freyr’s answer.

The blade may call Osric toward kingship, war, judgment, sacrifice, or leadership. It cannot make him wise before he has learned, healed before he has grieved, mature before he has grown, or ready merely because destiny has arrived.

Odin might argue that winter does not ask whether a field is ready.

Freyr can answer that winter cannot make it harvest.

Thus Odin wins the narrow point: Osric experiences Barrow’s Edge as destiny.

Freyr preserves the broader point: destiny does not complete the man it summons.

---

The eventual judgment should not fully vindicate Osric, because he cannot explain everything that happened.

The truth is too tangled.

His family died after elderberry syrup was insufficiently boiled because a hidden hearthstone collapse drew coals away into an ash hollow. The syrup remained warm, steamed, thickened, and appeared prepared, but the toxins were not adequately broken down. Nobody knowingly poisoned anyone. Osric later opened the bottle, unintentionally removing the gas-warning Ealhild might have recognized. She served it during the child’s cough. The family died.

The house was later destroyed through a sequence of collapse, fire, weather, and neglect that no surviving person can narrate in full.

The grain accusations arose around the same disaster.

Osric’s obsessive concern for the livestock was twisted into accusations of unnatural acts.

Every innocent circumstance became sinister because no one could see the complete chain.

The lord’s central judgment can therefore be:

> “Osric, I condemn you as a man who cannot explain his circumstances. Nothing more.”

That sentence matters enormously.

It refuses to call him murderer.

It refuses to call him thief.

It refuses to call him arsonist.

It refuses to call him beast-fouler.

It also refuses to pretend that all uncertainty has been resolved.

Osric’s tragedy is that his circumstances remain inexplicable even when he is not guilty of the horrors alleged. The lord can recognize that lack of proof without restoring Osric to his former place as though nothing happened.

The judgment should settle every remaining claim, prevent further feud, and remove Osric from the social world that can no longer contain him.

---

The house should be rebuilt.

The lord may order Osric to repair or rebuild the ruined home before leaving Hexham’s lands. This is both punishment and settlement. Osric cannot abandon a destroyed holding within his lord’s territory. He must restore its economic and social usefulness.

The cruelty is intimate. Osric must rebuild the place where his family died, make the walls sound, repair the hearth, restore the roof, and leave it fit for another household.

He gives the house a future from which he is excluded.

The lord can say:

> “The house you will rebuild.”

If Osric asks who will receive it, the lord may answer:

> “That is no longer your concern.”

Osric might answer:

> “It was my concern when it burned.”

The lord’s response:

> “And it remains your concern until it stands.”

Once the house is restored, Osric will be banished from Hexham’s lands.

This should not necessarily be full outlawry, which would strip him of legal protection and permit his killing without consequence. The intended sentence is exile or banishment under the lord’s peace: Osric leaves free, but loses his standing, his claim to remain, and his place within the local order.

> “When the house is restored, you will leave my lands. Your standing here is no more. Remain, and you will be no more.”

The final phrase makes the threat unmistakable. After the appointed time, remaining or returning will be treated as defiance punishable by death.

Osric receives closure, but not restoration.

His name is not officially condemned as monstrous.

His freedom is restored.

His old life is not.

---

Ealhild’s four cows should be returned to her father in Piceringas.

These cattle came through Ealhild’s dowry and are among the last living remnants of her household. They should not simply become the property of Hexham, because that would make the lord appear to enrich himself through the destruction of Osric’s family.

Returning them to Ealhild’s kin resolves the property claim, acknowledges their connection to her, and becomes a small diplomatic gesture toward the Swedish community at Piceringas near York.

This is especially important because Osric has accused his lord of sending Hexham’s men to war against Northumbrian neighbors in order to settle a debt owed to King Edmund.

The lord can answer that accusation not through confession, but through policy.

> “Let the cows be delivered to Piceringas, with the sorrow of the Lord of Hexham. Tell them that whatever banners stood against one another at Leicester, Hexham deals fairly with its neighbors.”

The wording should sound like lordly diplomacy rather than a modern public apology.

“My sorrow,” “my regret,” “my peace,” or “my assurance of fair dealing” fit better than “the sincerest apologies,” unless the lord is consciously making an unusually personal gesture.

A fuller version:

> “The four cows that came with Ealhild shall be driven to her father in Piceringas. My retainer will travel with them. Carry my sorrow for his daughter, and tell him this: banners may divide Hexham from York for a season, but Hexham does not enrich itself through the grief of its neighbors.”

This action becomes the lord’s partial acknowledgment that Osric spoke beyond his standing, but not beyond all truth.

The lord need not admit that sending the fyrd was wrong. He can demonstrate that military allegiance has not erased the obligations of neighborhood, trade, and fair dealing.

The cows began as a bond between Ealhild’s Swedish family and Osric’s household. Returning them restores that bond rather than allowing war, death, and accusation to turn them into spoils.

---

The lost grain claim should be settled through Osric’s field, but not through Osric’s labor.

The lord cannot prove that Osric stole his neighbors’ grain. The neighbors cannot recover their loss through certainty alone. Yet the missing grain remains a real grievance that could feed feud for years.

The solution is to let the field answer the claim for three seasons.

However, the neighbors who accuse Osric must work the field themselves.

The logic is:

> If they truly believe the field owes them grain, let them demonstrate that conviction by the sweat of their brows.

This prevents them from receiving compensation while Osric labors beneath an accusation the lord has not proved. It also tests the seriousness of their claim. Men who genuinely believe they suffered a substantial loss may be willing to plough, sow, weed, reap, thresh, and cart. Men motivated primarily by hatred may become less certain when the accusation requires labor.

The lord might say:

> “For three harvests, Osric’s field shall answer the grain claimed by his neighbors.”

The accusers may murmur approval.

Then:

> “But Osric will neither plough nor sow it for them. Those who say its yield is owed to them will work the earth themselves. Let their conviction be measured by the sweat of their brows.”

The murmuring stops.

The practical arrangement should reserve seed for each following season and account for whatever customary lordly due is owed. The remaining produce is divided among the households that brought the claim.

Participation can be voluntary but binding:

> “Any claimant who labors in the field shall receive his share. Any claimant who does not shall surrender his claim.”

Most importantly, taking the harvest must extinguish the grievance.

> “When the third harvest is gathered, no man shall name the grain against Osric again.”

Otherwise, the neighbors might take three years of produce and continue calling him thief.

The lord should make clear that this is not a formal conviction for theft.

> “Osric will rebuild the house. He will not labor under an accusation I have not proved.”

The barley settlement is not properly a wergild. Wergild is the assessed value attached to the killing or injury of a person. This is closer to compensation, amends, or a lord-imposed settlement of an unresolved property claim. Cynewulf might call it *bōt*, though “barley-bōt” would be more likely a character’s convenient term than an attested technical category.

The lord himself need not name it. He simply pronounces what the field, the accusers, and the seasons must do.

---

The judgment can therefore unfold in this general shape:

> “Osric, I condemn you as a man who cannot explain his circumstances. Nothing more.
>
> “You are not named murderer in this hall. You are not named thief. You are not named destroyer of your own house, nor defiler of the beasts entrusted to you. These things have been spoken. They have not been proved.
>
> “But neither can all that happened beneath your roof be answered. Your household is dead. Your neighbors’ grain is gone. Your home lies broken. Blood has followed accusation, and accusation has become feud.
>
> “The house you will rebuild.
>
> “The four cows that came with Ealhild shall be returned to her father in Piceringas. My retainer Galindo will carry my sorrow for his daughter and my assurance that Hexham deals fairly with its neighbors, whatever banners stood at Leicester.
>
> “For three harvests, your field shall answer the grain claimed by your neighbors. But you will not work it for them. Those who maintain the claim will plough, sow, and reap for themselves. Let their conviction be known by the sweat of their brows. Seed shall be kept. The customary due shall be rendered. The remainder shall be divided among those who labor. When the third harvest is taken, their claim against you is ended.
>
> “When the house stands and the third harvest is gathered, you will leave Hexham’s lands.
>
> “Your standing here is no more.
>
> “Remain beyond the appointed day, and you will be no more.”

This judgment is harsh, but it is not corrupt.

The lord preserves the value of the holding.

He settles the grain grievance without naming Osric thief.

He returns Ealhild’s property to her kin.

He makes a diplomatic gesture toward Piceringas.

He prevents himself or Hexham from profiting from the deaths.

He ends the neighbors’ legal claim.

He keeps Osric alive and free.

He removes Osric from a community in which hatred has become too entrenched for peace.

He acknowledges uncertainty without allowing uncertainty to become permanent feud.

Osric loses his home, cattle, standing, community, and future in Hexham. Yet he receives what he came for: a public distinction between accusation and proof.

The lord does not restore Osric’s old life.

He declares that the worst stories about him have not earned the authority of judgment.

That may be enough.

When asked whether he understands and will obey, Osric might answer:

> “I came here too.”

That completes the choice made at the end of the first book. Osric did not come to Hexham expecting mercy, triumph, or vindication. He came because judgment, even a painful judgment, was better than allowing other men to finish writing his life for him.

---

Later scene, the guard who confiscates Barrow's Edge stares at the unique blade in its scabbard.

While Ingvarr occupies the guard’s body, the guard’s awareness does not merely vanish. It is displaced to the edge of Alfheimr... or to whatever threshold lies between the living world and the place from which the barrow-spirits keep watch.

He finds himself somewhere impossibly warm and ordinary:

> Summer sunlight.
> A timber house.
> A threshold stone beneath his boots.
> A locked door.

He has no idea whose home it is. The door will not admit him because he is not dead, not invited, or simply not meant to cross. Then his heart resumes, and he is thrown back into his body.

The locked door is especially good. It implies that he came dangerously close to somewhere, but never gained entry. The threshold stone makes the image exact: he was neither wholly alive in his body nor received among the dead.

The sequence could read:

> The guard drew Barrow’s Edge the width of a finger.
>
> Dark iron showed beneath the mouth of the sheath.
>
> He drew another inch.
>
> His heart struck once.
>
> Then...
>
> Summer.
>
> He stood upon the threshold stone of a timber house.
>
> Warm sunlight lay across the yard. Long grass stirred beside a low fence, and somewhere beyond the house, cattle moved lazily beneath the trees.
>
> The door stood before him.
>
> He reached for its iron latch.
>
> It would not lift.
>
> He tried again.
>
> Locked.
>
> From within came the faint sound of movement. A chair shifting, perhaps. A boot upon the floor.
>
> He raised his hand to knock.
>
> His heart slammed into his chest.
>
> The hall returned around him.
>
> Barrow’s Edge lay sheathed upon the table.
>
> It was no longer in his hands.
>
> The guard staggered backward. Pain tightened beneath his breastbone, sharp enough to steal his breath, then loosened almost as quickly.
>
> He stared at the sword.
>
> He remembered drawing the first inch.
>
> He remembered sunlight.
>
> He remembered a locked door.
>
> He did not remember putting the blade away.

Then the reader can see what he cannot:

> In the silence between heartbeats, scarred shadow-hands had closed over his and guided the iron home.

His panic now has multiple causes. Someone moved his body. His heart failed briefly. He stood somewhere that could not exist. Something was inside the house... and the door very deliberately refused him.

The ten-step denial becomes even funnier afterward:

> He ran.
>
> After ten paces, he stopped and looked back.
>
> The sword remained sheathed.
>
> No summer light spilled beneath the hall door.
>
> He shook his head once.
>
> Then he continued running.

The image also answers book-one questions without fully explaining them. Possession does not leave the living consciousness watching helplessly from inside the body. It pushes that consciousness onto a threshold elsewhere. Whether that threshold is truly Alfheimr, a memory belonging to Ingvarr, or merely the shape the mind gives to a stopped heart can remain unresolved.

And the guard will never report the full truth. Later, when asked why he ran:

> “Perhaps I saw… a shadow.”

He certainly will not mention that the shadow had a house.
